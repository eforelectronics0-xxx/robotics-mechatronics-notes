26Trajectory Planning and Generation

How a robot decides where to be at every instant between two taught points: path and trajectory, joint space versus Cartesian space, velocity profiles, and polynomial interpolation.

1.Path, Trajectory and the Planning Problem

Definition

A path is the set of geometric points in space that the tool must pass through, without any reference to time. A trajectory is a path with a time schedule attached to it — it specifies where the tool is at every instant, and therefore also its velocity and acceleration. Trajectory planning is the process of generating a trajectory that moves the robot from an initial state to a final state, satisfying the geometric, kinematic and dynamic constraints of the task and of the machine.

The problem arises because the operator only teaches a small number of points. Between two consecutive taught points the robot may have to travel 500 mm, and if the controller simply commanded a jump from one to the other the result would be a violent, uncontrolled movement. The controller must therefore interpolate: it must compute a smooth, continuous set of intermediate positions at a high rate (typically 1 kHz to 4 kHz for the servo loop and 100 Hz to 1 kHz for the interpolator), while respecting the limits of velocity, acceleration, jerk, motor torque and the workspace.

Constraints that must be satisfied

  • Boundary conditions — the position, velocity and sometimes acceleration at the start and at the end of the move must match what is required (usually zero velocity at both ends).
  • Kinematic limits — maximum joint velocity, maximum joint acceleration and maximum tool speed.
  • Dynamic limits — maximum motor torque, which depends on inertia, gravity, friction and the payload.
  • Geometric constraints — the path must avoid obstacles, must stay inside the workspace, and must avoid singularities and joint limits.
  • Process constraints — in welding, painting or grinding the tool speed along the path must be constant and correct for the process.
  • Smoothness — the velocity and preferably the acceleration must be continuous, otherwise vibration, noise, wear and poor surface finish result.
  • Optimality — among all feasible trajectories, the fastest or the most energy-efficient is preferred.

2.Joint Space versus Cartesian Space Planning

Joint space (joint interpolated) motion

Each joint is planned independently, using a polynomial that takes it from its initial angle to its final angle. The joints may reach their targets at the same time but they do not move in a coordinated way in space, so the path of the tool is generally a curved and unpredictable line. This is the classic point-to-point (PTP) or joint move, called MOVJ in many robot languages and MoveJ in RAPID.

  • Advantages: the computation is very simple because each joint is planned independently; no inverse kinematics is needed; there is no risk of a singularity; it is the fastest way to get from one pose to another because every joint can use its full speed; it never fails, since a joint move always has a solution.
  • Disadvantages: the path of the tool is unknown, so it may collide with a fixture or with itself; the tool orientation changes in an uncontrolled way; the process speed along the path cannot be controlled; it is therefore unsuitable for any process that depends on the path.
  • Use: rapid traverses between operations, pick and place, spot welding, machine loading and any motion where only the end points matter.

Cartesian space (linear or circular interpolated) motion

The desired path is defined in the workspace — a straight line, a circle or a spline — and the controller computes the tool position at each interpolation interval along that path, then converts each position into joint angles using inverse kinematics. In robot languages this is MOVL / MoveL for a straight line and MOVC / MoveC for a circular arc through three points.

  • Advantages: the tool follows exactly the required geometric path at the required speed, which is essential for welding, cutting, painting, gluing and grinding; the path is predictable, so collision checking is straightforward.
  • Disadvantages: the computation is heavy because inverse kinematics must be solved at every interpolation point; the path may pass through a singularity, where the required joint velocities become infinite; the required joint velocities may exceed the limits near the edge of the workspace; the move may fail if no inverse kinematic solution exists for some point on the line.
  • Use: arc welding, laser and waterjet cutting, spray painting, adhesive dispensing, grinding, polishing, deburring, milling and any contour following task.

3.Velocity Profiles (Speed Planning Along a Path)

Once the geometric path is decided, the second question is how fast to travel along it. This is described by the velocity profile. The aim is to complete the move in the shortest time while keeping the velocity, acceleration and jerk within limits and while making the motion as smooth as possible.

Trapezoidal velocity profile

The simplest and most widely used profile. The motion has three phases: accelerate at a constant rate to the maximum velocity, cruise at constant velocity, and decelerate at a constant rate to rest. If the move is too short to reach the maximum velocity, the cruise phase disappears and the profile becomes triangular.

With acceleration a, maximum velocity vmax and total distance D:
time to reach vmax: ta = vmax/a , distance during acceleration = vmax²/(2a)
If D < vmax²/a the profile is triangular and the peak velocity is vpeak = √(a D)

The disadvantage is that the acceleration changes instantly at the start, at the two transitions and at the end. An instantaneous change of acceleration is an infinite jerk, which excites the natural frequencies of the structure and produces vibration, noise, wear and poor surface finish.

S-curve (jerk-limited) velocity profile

The acceleration itself is ramped up and down smoothly, so the velocity curve looks like an S. There are seven phases: increasing acceleration, constant acceleration, decreasing acceleration, constant velocity, decreasing deceleration, constant deceleration, increasing deceleration. The jerk (rate of change of acceleration) is limited to a set value. This greatly reduces vibration and machine wear and improves surface finish in machining and painting, at the cost of slightly longer move time and more computation.

Polynomial position profiles

Instead of specifying the velocity profile directly, the position can be specified as a polynomial in time, and the velocity and acceleration follow by differentiation.

Formula

Cubic polynomial (4 coefficients, 4 conditions)θ(t) = a₀ + a₁t + a₂t² + a₃t³Conditions: θ(0) = θ₀, θ(tf) = θf, θ̇(0) = 0, θ̇(tf) = 0. Solving gives:
θ(t) = θ₀ + (θf − θ₀) [ 3(t/tf)² − 2(t/tf)³ ]Velocity is a parabola with a maximum of 1.5(θf−θ₀)/tf at the mid-point; acceleration is a straight line and is discontinuous at t = 0 and t = tf, so there is a jerk impulse at each end.

Quintic polynomial (6 coefficients, 6 conditions)θ(t) = a₀ + a₁t + a₂t² + a₃t³ + a₎t⁴ + aₑt⁵Conditions: θ, θ̇ and θ̈ are all specified at both the start and the end. Solving for zero initial and final velocity and acceleration gives:
θ(t) = θ₀ + (θf − θ₀) [ 10τ³ − 15τ⁴ + 6τ⁵ ] , where τ = t/tfThe peak velocity is 1.875(θf−θ₀)/tf and the peak acceleration is 5.77(θf−θ₀)/tf². Position, velocity and acceleration are all continuous — the smoothest of the simple profiles.

Example

Numerical. A joint must move from 10° to 60° in 2 seconds using a cubic profile. θ(t) = 10 + 50[3(t/2)² − 2(t/2)³]. At t = 1 s, τ = t/tf = 0.5, so θ = 10 + 50[3(0.25) − 2(0.125)] = 10 + 50[0.75 − 0.25] = 10 + 25 = 35°. The maximum velocity occurs at t = 1 s and equals 1.5 × 50/2 = 37.5 °/s. The acceleration at the start is 6(θf−θ₀)/tf² = 6 × 50/4 = 75 °/s² and it changes sign abruptly at t = 1 s, which is the weakness of the cubic profile.

Other interpolation methods

  • Linear interpolation with parabolic blends — constant velocity in the middle of each segment with a parabolic (constant acceleration) blend at each end. Very common in CNC and in robot controllers.
  • Higher-order splines — cubic splines give continuous position, velocity and acceleration through all the via points.
  • Trigonometric (cycloidal) motion — θ(t) = θ₀ + (θf−θ₀)[t/tf − (1/2π)sin(2πt/tf)]. Acceleration is a smooth sine, so there is no jerk at all; used in high-speed cam design and in precision machines.
  • Bang-bang (time-optimal) control — accelerate at the maximum possible rate and then decelerate at the maximum possible rate. This gives the shortest possible move time but the harshest motion.
  • Look-ahead (block transition) smoothing — in CNC, the controller reads many blocks ahead and blends the corners so that the machine does not have to stop at every line end.

4.Trajectory Generation in a Robot Controller

  1. Teaching. The operator records a series of points, each with a position, an orientation, a motion type (joint, linear or circular), a speed and a blend (cornering) tolerance.
  2. Path definition. The controller builds the geometric path from the sequence of points.
  3. Velocity planning. The controller decides the speed along each segment and blends the corners, subject to the joint velocity and acceleration limits and to the commanded speed.
  4. Interpolation. At each interpolation cycle (for example every 4 ms) the controller computes the next commanded tool position and orientation along the path.
  5. Inverse kinematics. Each commanded tool pose is converted into a set of joint angles.
  6. Joint-level trajectory generation. For joint moves, the joint polynomials are evaluated directly instead.
  7. Servo loop. At a much higher rate (for example every 0.25 ms to 1 ms) each joint servo compares its actual encoder position with the commanded position and applies a PID or computed-torque correction.
  8. Monitoring. Following error, torque, temperature, limit switches and singularities are monitored continuously, and the motion is stopped if any limit is exceeded.
Note

Blending (cornering). When a robot moves through a series of points, stopping exactly at each one wastes a great deal of time. Controllers therefore allow the path to cut the corner within a specified tolerance, called the zone, blend radius or approximation distance. A large zone gives a fast, smooth motion but deviates from the taught point; a zone of zero gives an exact stop at the point. In continuous-path processes such as welding the zone must usually be zero or very small; in handling it can be large.

Common problems in trajectory generation

  • Singularity avoidance — near a singularity the joint velocities required for a given tool velocity become very large. Controllers reduce the tool speed automatically or refuse the move.
  • Joint limit avoidance — the inverse kinematics may return a solution in which a joint is beyond its limit; the controller must select a different solution branch.
  • Solution branch selection — a six-axis robot generally has up to eight inverse kinematic solutions for a given pose. The controller must choose the one nearest to the current configuration and must not switch branches in the middle of a path, which would cause a large unexpected movement.
  • Wrist flip and elbow flip — undesirable branch changes; avoided by checking the continuity of the joint angles.
  • Following error — the difference between the commanded and the actual position, caused by friction, inertia and compliance. It grows with speed and is a fundamental limit on accuracy.
  • Collision detection — the trajectory must be checked against the fixtures, the machine and the robot itself, usually by off-line simulation.

★Summary

  • A path is geometry only; a trajectory is a path with a time schedule, giving position, velocity and acceleration at every instant.
  • Trajectory planning must satisfy boundary conditions, kinematic and dynamic limits, geometric and process constraints, smoothness and, if possible, optimality.
  • Joint space planning moves each joint independently; the tool path is unpredictable but the computation is simple, no singularity can occur, and it is the fastest way between two poses.
  • Cartesian space planning follows an exact straight line or arc; it needs inverse kinematics at every interpolation point and risks singularities and joint limit violations.
  • A trapezoidal velocity profile has constant acceleration, cruise and deceleration phases; it is simple but produces infinite jerk at the transitions.
  • An S-curve profile limits the jerk and gives much smoother, quieter motion with less wear.
  • A cubic polynomial gives continuous position and velocity but discontinuous acceleration; a quintic polynomial gives continuous position, velocity and acceleration.
  • Cubic blend: θ(t) = θ₀ + (θf−θ₀)[3τ² − 2τ³]; quintic blend: θ(t) = θ₀ + (θf−θ₀)[10τ³ − 15τ⁴ + 6τ⁵].
  • Blending (cornering tolerance) lets the robot cut corners within a set tolerance, greatly reducing cycle time.
  • The controller performs path definition, velocity planning, interpolation, inverse kinematics and a much faster servo loop.
