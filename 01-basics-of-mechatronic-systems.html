01Basics of Mechatronic Systems

What mechatronics really means, the key elements that make up a mechatronic system, the design process an engineer follows, and worked examples such as a washing machine and an anti-lock braking system.

1.What is Mechatronics?

The word mechatronics was first used in 1969 by Tetsuro Mori of the Japanese company Yaskawa Electric. It is formed from two words, mecha from mechanism and tronics from electronics. In simple language, mechatronics is the synergistic integration of mechanical engineering, electronics, control theory and computer programming in the design of a product or a process.

The important word here is integration. In the old style of design, a mechanical engineer designed the mechanism, an electrical engineer added a motor and a switch, and the two parts were joined at the end. In mechatronic design, all these disciplines are considered together from the very first day. The mechanism is designed keeping the sensor and the controller in mind, and the controller software is written keeping the mechanism in mind. The result is a product that is simpler, cheaper, lighter, more reliable and far more flexible than the same product built the traditional way.

Definition

Mechatronics is the design philosophy in which mechanical systems, sensors, actuators, electronics and computer control are integrated from the concept stage itself so that the final product performs a task with greater precision, flexibility and intelligence than a purely mechanical or purely electronic design could achieve.

Note

A useful way to remember the idea: Mechatronics = Mechanics + Electronics + Control + Computing. If any one of these four is missing, the system is not truly mechatronic.

2.Key Elements of a Mechatronic System

Every mechatronic system, whether it is a simple automatic water cooler or a complex industrial robot, contains the same five groups of elements. The physical process is sensed, the signal is cleaned up, a decision is taken, an action is produced, and the effect of that action is sensed again. This chain is shown below.

1. Sensors and Transducers

Sensors are the eyes, ears and skin of the system. A sensor responds to a physical quantity such as temperature, position, force, light, pressure or speed and produces a corresponding electrical signal, usually a voltage, a current, a change in resistance or a change in capacitance. The device that performs this energy conversion is called a transducer. For example, in an automatic washing machine a pressure switch senses the water level and a thermistor senses the water temperature.

2. Signal Conditioning

The raw output of a sensor is rarely in a form that a controller can use directly. It may be only a few millivolts, it may be sitting on top of electrical noise, or it may be an analogue value while the controller works in digital. Signal conditioning is the stage that converts the sensor output into a clean, properly scaled, usable signal. It normally includes the following operations.

  • Amplification — an instrumentation amplifier raises a few millivolts to a few volts.
  • Filtering — low-pass, high-pass or band-pass circuits remove unwanted noise, especially the 50 Hz mains hum.
  • Linearisation — many sensors are non-linear; a circuit or a software look-up table makes the output proportional to the measured quantity.
  • Bridge completion and balancing — needed for strain gauges and other resistive sensors.
  • Analogue to digital conversion (ADC) — converts the analogue voltage into a binary number the microcontroller can read.
  • Isolation — opto-couplers protect the low-voltage electronics from the high-voltage power side.

3. Digital Control System

This is the brain of the mechatronic system. It receives the digital values from the signal conditioning stage, compares them with the desired values (the set point), decides what action is required and sends commands to the actuators. Depending on the complexity of the task, the controller may be a simple microcontroller such as the ATmega328 or the 8051, a Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) in a factory, a Digital Signal Processor (DSP) where fast mathematics is needed, or a full industrial computer. The intelligence of the system lies in the program stored in its memory.

4. Actuators

If sensors are the sense organs, actuators are the muscles. An actuator converts the electrical command from the controller back into physical action — motion, force, heat or light. Common actuators are DC motors, stepper motors, servo motors, AC induction motors, solenoids, pneumatic and hydraulic cylinders, heaters, valves and relays. The choice of actuator depends on the required speed, torque, accuracy, cost and power source.

5. The Physical System / Process

This is the mechanical plant that actually does the work — the drum of a washing machine, the wheels of a car, the arm of a robot, the water in a tank. Everything else in the mechatronic system exists to make this physical system behave in the desired manner.

Note

Besides these five, every real system also needs a power supply (often more than one, for example 24 V for the actuators and 5 V for the logic) and some form of display or user interface such as LEDs, a seven-segment display or an LCD screen.

3.The Mechatronic Design Process

Designing a mechatronic system is not a straight line from idea to product. It is an iterative process, which means the designer keeps going back and improving earlier decisions whenever a later test reveals a problem. The following stages are generally followed.

  1. Identify the need. Everything begins with a problem or a requirement, for example “we need a machine that fills exactly 500 ml of liquid into bottles at 60 bottles per minute.”
  2. Define the problem and write the specification. The vague need is turned into measurable numbers: accuracy ±2 ml, speed 60 bottles/min, cost below a certain limit, power available, space available, safety standards to be met.
  3. Generate concepts. Several possible solutions are drawn on paper. Should the filler use a piston pump, a gravity tank or a peristaltic pump? Should position be sensed with an encoder or a limit switch? All ideas are listed without rejecting any at this stage.
  4. Model and analyse. The chosen concepts are analysed mathematically and by simulation. Free-body diagrams, circuit diagrams, transfer functions and CAD models are made. Software such as MATLAB/Simulink, Proteus or Multisim helps to check the idea before any money is spent on hardware.
  5. Build a prototype. A working model is assembled, usually on a breadboard with a development board and simple mechanical parts.
  6. Test, evaluate and refine. The prototype is tested against the specification written in stage 2. Whatever fails is corrected. The loop repeats until the specification is fully met.
  7. Final design, documentation and manufacture. Drawings, circuit schematics, program listings, a bill of material, a user manual and maintenance instructions are prepared so that the product can be manufactured and supported.
Note

Notice that the specification is written before the design starts. This is the single most important discipline in mechatronic design, because without a written specification there is no objective way to decide whether the finished product is good or bad.

Case study 1 — The automatic washing machine

A fully automatic washing machine is one of the clearest examples of a mechatronic system because it uses every key element. The user selects a program on the input panel; this is the command input. A microcontroller reads the program and starts the sequence. First it opens the water inlet solenoid valve and waits until the water level sensor (usually a pressure switch or a float switch) reports that the tub is full. Then it switches on the heater if a warm wash is selected and monitors a thermistor until the set temperature is reached. Next the motor turns the drum alternately clockwise and anticlockwise for the wash period, and the drain pump empties the tub. Finally the motor spins the drum at high speed to remove water. Throughout the cycle the door lock switch is monitored; if the door is opened the controller stops everything for safety.

Notice what makes this system mechatronic rather than merely electromechanical: the wash sequence is stored as software. Adding a new wash program, changing the spin speed or making the machine decide the water quantity from the weight of the clothes requires no new mechanical parts — only a change in the program. This flexibility is the great advantage of mechatronics.

Case study 2 — Anti-lock braking system (ABS)

When a driver brakes hard on a slippery road, the wheels can lock and stop rotating. A locked wheel has almost no sideways grip, so the vehicle skids and the driver loses steering control. ABS prevents this. A wheel speed sensor at each wheel (a Hall sensor or a variable reluctance sensor working with a toothed ring) continuously reports the rotational speed. The electronic control unit compares the four speeds. If one wheel begins to decelerate much faster than the others, which means it is about to lock, the ECU commands the hydraulic modulator to release the brake pressure on that wheel and then re-apply it. This happens ten to twenty times every second, far faster than any human could pump the brake pedal. The driver feels a pulsing in the pedal and hears a clicking sound, which is normal.

ABS shows another important mechatronic benefit. The mechanical braking system is unchanged; intelligence has been added purely through sensors, electronics and software. The same idea is used in electronic stability control, traction control and cruise control.

★Summary

  • Mechatronics is the integrated design of mechanics, electronics, control and computing, considered together from the concept stage.
  • The five key elements are sensors/transducers, signal conditioning, the digital control system, actuators and the physical system, supported by a power supply.
  • Signal conditioning includes amplification, filtering, linearisation, isolation and analogue-to-digital conversion.
  • The design process is iterative: identify the need, define the problem and specification, generate concepts, model and analyse, prototype, test and refine, then finalise.
  • The specification must be written before design begins so that the finished product can be judged objectively.
  • The washing machine and ABS are classic examples: in both, the mechanical hardware is simple and most of the intelligence is provided by sensors, a microcontroller and software.
  • The main advantage is flexibility; the main disadvantage is the need for multi-disciplinary skills and a dependable power supply.
