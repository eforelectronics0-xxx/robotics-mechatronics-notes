09Electrical Actuation Systems — Mechanical and Solid State Switches

How a mechatronic system switches power on and off: mechanical switches with their contacts, ratings and bounce, and solid state switches built from transistors, MOSFETs, triacs, relays and opto-isolators.

On this page

  1. Why Switches are Needed
  2. Mechanical Switches
  3. Solid State Switches

1.Why Switches are Needed

A controller output from a microcontroller pin can supply only a few tens of milliamperes at 5 V. An actuator such as a motor, a solenoid, a heater or a lamp may need several amperes at 12 V, 24 V or even 230 V AC. A switch is the device that sits between the two and allows a small control signal to control a much larger power flow. Switches are of two broad kinds: mechanical switches, which have moving contacts, and solid state switches, which have no moving parts and use semiconductor devices.

2.Mechanical Switches

Definition

A mechanical switch is an electromechanical device in which two or more metal contacts are brought together or separated by a mechanical force, thereby making or breaking an electric circuit.

Switch terminology

  • Pole — the number of separate circuits the switch controls. Single pole (SP), double pole (DP), three pole (3P).
  • Throw — the number of connections each pole can make. Single throw (ST) has one connection; double throw (DT) has two.
  • Normally open (NO) — the contacts are open when the switch is not operated; they close when it is pressed. Also called a make contact.
  • Normally closed (NC) — the contacts are closed when the switch is not operated; they open when it is pressed. Also called a break contact.
  • Changeover (CO) or break-before-make — a common terminal that switches between an NC and an NO contact.
  • Momentary — the switch returns to its normal state when released, as in a push button. Maintained (latching) — it stays in the new position, as in a toggle or rocker switch.
  • Contact rating — the maximum voltage and current the contacts can switch, for example 250 V AC / 6 A or 30 V DC / 5 A. DC ratings are always lower than AC ratings because a DC arc is not extinguished naturally at each zero crossing.
  • Contact resistance — the small resistance of closed contacts, typically 10 mΩ to 100 mΩ. It grows as the contacts corrode.
  • Insulation resistance — the resistance between open contacts and to the body, typically over 100 MΩ.
  • Dielectric strength — the voltage the open contacts can withstand without breaking down.
  • Electrical life — the number of operations under rated load, typically 104 to 106. Mechanical life without load is much higher, up to 107 to 108.

Types of mechanical switch

  • Push button — momentary, NO or NC. Used for start/stop commands.
  • Toggle and rocker — maintained, used for mains ON/OFF.
  • Slide switch — maintained, common in small battery-powered equipment.
  • Rotary and wafer switch — selects one of many positions, as in a range selector.
  • Limit switch — operated by the movement of a machine part; used to detect the end of travel in machine tools, lifts and robot joints. Often has a roller lever arm.
  • Proximity switch — contactless operation, but its output is usually a relay contact.
  • Microswitch — a very small snap-action switch with a precise operating point and a short travel; used inside appliances, in mouse buttons and as end stops.
  • Reed switch — two thin ferromagnetic reeds sealed in a glass tube with an inert gas; a nearby magnet closes them. Used in float switches, door alarms and cylinder position sensing.
  • Relay — a switch operated electromagnetically rather than by hand (described below).
  • Mercury switch — a pool of mercury makes or breaks contact as the tube tilts; used for tilt detection, now largely replaced for environmental reasons.
  • Selector, key-operated and emergency stop (mushroom head) switches — used in industrial control panels. An emergency stop is always NC so that a broken wire causes a safe shutdown.

Contact bounce

When two metal contacts close they do not settle immediately. They bounce apart and together several times over a period of 1 ms to 10 ms because of their elasticity and the impact. To a human this is invisible, but to a microcontroller running at millions of instructions per second it looks like the switch being pressed many times. This causes wrong counts, skipped states and erratic behaviour.

  • Hardware debouncing — an RC low-pass filter (for example 10 kΩ and 100 nF, giving τ = 1 ms) followed by a Schmitt trigger such as the 74HC14; or an SR latch built from two NAND gates, which responds to the first edge only and then ignores everything until the contacts settle.
  • Software debouncing — read the input, wait 10 ms to 30 ms, read it again, and accept the value only if both readings agree. Alternatively, use a timer interrupt to sample the input every 5 ms and require the same value in three consecutive samples.
  • Mechanical reduction — use a snap-action switch, add a small capacitor of 10 nF to 100 nF directly across the contacts, or use a mercury-wetted contact.

Relay

A relay is an electrically operated switch. A coil of many turns wound on a soft iron core produces a magnetic field when current flows. The field attracts a soft iron armature which carries the moving contact, so the contact closes (or opens). When the coil is de-energised a spring returns the armature.

Relays are widely used because they provide complete galvanic isolation between the low-voltage control circuit and the high-voltage load circuit, and they can switch both AC and DC with the same contacts. Typical ratings are 12 V DC coil and 250 V AC / 10 A contacts. A latching relay has two coils or a permanent magnet so that it holds its position without continuous coil current, saving power.

Important

A relay coil is a large inductor. When the current is switched off, the collapsing magnetic field generates a voltage spike of several hundred volts that can destroy the driving transistor. A flyback (freewheeling) diode such as a 1N4007 must always be connected in reverse bias across the coil. For AC coils a varistor (MOV) or an RC snubber is used instead.

Advantages and disadvantages of mechanical switches

  • Advantages: very low on-resistance, so almost no voltage drop and no heat; complete isolation when open; can switch AC or DC, high voltage and high current; immune to electromagnetic interference and to static discharge; simple, cheap and easy to understand; no leakage current when off.
  • Disadvantages: slow (5 ms to 20 ms operating time); mechanical wear limits the life; contacts bounce; arcing causes erosion, radio interference and a fire risk in explosive atmospheres; larger and heavier than a semiconductor; sensitive to dust, moisture and vibration; cannot be switched at high frequency.

3.Solid State Switches

Definition

A solid state switch is an electronic switch built from semiconductor devices such as a diode, a bipolar transistor, a MOSFET, an IGBT, a thyristor, a TRIAC or a photo-coupled device. It has no moving parts and no contacts.

Bipolar junction transistor (BJT) as a switch

A transistor works as a switch between two states: cut-off (no base current, so no collector current — the switch is OFF and the collector voltage equals the supply) and saturation (enough base current that the collector-emitter voltage falls to about 0.2 V — the switch is ON). The active region in between is used for amplification, not for switching.

To ensure saturation, the base current must satisfy IB ≥ IC(sat) / β, and in practice a forced β of 10 is used for reliable switching. For example, to switch a 12 V relay coil of 100 Ω (IC = 120 mA) using a BC548 with β = 100, IB should be at least 12 mA, and the base resistor from a 5 V microcontroller pin is RB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.012 = 358 Ω, so 330 Ω is used.

IB = (Vcontrol − VBE) / RB , VBE ≈ 0.7 V , IC(sat) = VCC / Rload

MOSFET as a switch

A power MOSFET is now the preferred switch for DC loads. Its gate is insulated from the channel, so it draws essentially zero steady gate current — only the capacitance has to be charged each time it switches. When the gate-source voltage exceeds the threshold, the channel turns on and behaves like a small resistance RDS(on), typically a few milliohms. The conduction loss is I²RDS(on), which is very small.

  • A logic-level MOSFET such as the IRLZ44N turns fully on with a 4.5 V to 5 V gate drive and can therefore be driven directly from a microcontroller. A standard MOSFET such as the IRF540 needs 10 V and requires a gate driver.
  • An N-channel MOSFET is placed on the low side (between the load and ground), which is the simplest arrangement. A P-channel MOSFET is used for high-side switching.
  • A gate pull-down resistor of 10 kΩ to 100 kΩ keeps the MOSFET off while the microcontroller pin is floating during start-up.
  • Fast switching needs a low-impedance gate drive; a small series gate resistor of 10 Ω to 100 Ω damps ringing.
  • An inductive load such as a motor or a solenoid always needs a freewheeling diode across it.

IGBT

The Insulated Gate Bipolar Transistor combines the easy gate drive of a MOSFET with the low saturation voltage of a bipolar transistor. It is used for high voltage (600 V to 6500 V) and high current (tens to thousands of amperes) applications such as variable frequency drives for AC motors, traction, induction heating, welding sets and servo amplifiers. It switches at moderate frequency (a few kHz to 20 kHz).

Thyristor (SCR) and TRIAC

A thyristor (silicon controlled rectifier, SCR) is a four-layer device with three terminals: anode, cathode and gate. Once triggered by a short gate pulse it stays ON (latches) until the anode current falls below the holding current. It conducts in only one direction, so it is used for DC power control and for phase control of AC (two SCRs back-to-back). A TRIAC conducts in both directions and is therefore ideal for AC power control of lamps, heaters and small motors. A DIAC is used to trigger a TRIAC symmetrically, as in a light dimmer.

Opto-isolator and solid state relay (SSR)

An opto-isolator (opto-coupler) contains an LED and a phototransistor (or phototriac) in one package, coupled only by light. The input and output circuits are therefore completely electrically separated, with typical isolation ratings of 2500 V to 7500 V. This protects the delicate microcontroller from mains transients and from ground loops.

A solid state relay (SSR) combines an opto-isolator with a power triac or MOSFET and a heat sink in a single package, giving a device that looks like a relay from outside but has no moving parts. Mains SSRs usually include zero-crossing switching, which turns the triac on only at the instant the AC voltage passes through zero; this minimises inrush current and radio interference.

Advantages and disadvantages of solid state switches

  • Advantages: no moving parts, so very long life and high reliability; extremely fast switching, enabling pulse width modulation at kilohertz frequencies; silent operation; no contact bounce and no arcing; small size and light weight; immune to shock and vibration; low control power; can be integrated directly onto a printed circuit board; compatible with logic levels.
  • Disadvantages: a voltage drop when on (0.2 V for a BJT, 0.7 V to 2 V for a triac), so power is dissipated as heat and a heat sink is needed; leakage current flows even when off, so the load is never truly isolated; sensitive to over-voltage transients, over-current and over-temperature; can be damaged by static electricity; most types switch only AC or only DC, not both; failure is usually a short circuit, which is unsafe; generates electrical noise during fast switching.
Example

Design example. A microcontroller pin at 5 V must switch a 12 V, 24 W DC fan. Fan current I = 24/12 = 2 A. Choose an IRLZ44N logic-level N-channel MOSFET with RDS(on) = 22 mΩ. Conduction loss = I²R = 4 × 0.022 = 0.088 W — negligible, so no heat sink is needed. Connect the fan between +12 V and the drain, the source to ground, the gate to the microcontroller pin through 100 Ω, a 10 kΩ pull-down from gate to ground, and a 1N4007 diode in reverse bias across the fan to absorb the back-EMF when the fan is switched off.

★Summary

  • A switch lets a low-power control signal govern a high-power actuator circuit.
  • Switch terminology: pole (number of circuits), throw (number of connections), NO, NC, changeover, momentary and maintained.
  • Important switch ratings are contact voltage and current, contact resistance, insulation resistance, dielectric strength and electrical life.
  • Contact bounce lasts 1 ms to 10 ms and must be removed by an RC filter with a Schmitt trigger, by an SR latch, or by software re-reading after a delay.
  • A relay is an electrically operated switch giving complete isolation; its coil must always have a flyback diode to absorb the inductive voltage spike.
  • A BJT switch works between cut-off and saturation; design for IB ≥ IC/10.
  • A MOSFET switch needs no steady gate current, has very low RDS(on) and is preferred for DC loads; logic-level types can be driven directly from 5 V.
  • IGBTs are used for high-voltage high-current motor drives; SCRs and TRIACs are used for AC power control.
  • Opto-isolators and solid state relays give electrical isolation with no moving parts; SSRs use zero-crossing switching to reduce interference.
  • Mechanical switches have very low on-resistance and true isolation but are slow and wear out; solid state switches are fast and long-lived but drop voltage, leak current and usually fail short circuit.
