25Rotary to Linear Motion — Lead Screws, Rack and Pinion, Slider Cranks, Cams, Linkages and Couplers

The mechanisms that turn a motor's rotation into straight-line movement: lead and ball screws, rack and pinion, slider-crank, cams, four-bar linkages and their coupler curves, plus the couplings that connect them.

1.Lead Screws and Ball Screws

Definition

A lead screw (or power screw) converts rotary motion into linear motion by means of a helical thread. A nut travels along the screw as it rotates. The distance the nut moves in one complete revolution is called the lead. In a single-start thread the lead equals the pitch; in a multi-start thread the lead equals the pitch multiplied by the number of starts.

Thread forms

  • Square thread — theoretically the most efficient because the load is axial and there is no wedging action, but it is difficult to manufacture and cannot be adjusted for wear.
  • Acme / trapezoidal thread — a 29° included angle. Stronger than a square thread, easier to cut, and the nut can be adjusted for wear by a split nut. The most common form for plain lead screws.
  • Buttress thread — used where the load acts in one direction only, as in presses and jacks.
  • Ball screw thread — a gothic or circular arc profile designed to accommodate the recirculating balls.

Plain lead screw

The nut slides directly on the thread, so friction is sliding friction and the efficiency is only 30 % to 60 %. The advantage is that a plain lead screw with a small lead angle is self-locking: the friction is high enough that an axial load cannot back-drive the screw, so the position is held when the motor is switched off. This is very useful in a vertical axis, where it prevents the load from falling. Plain lead screws are cheap, quiet and simple, and are used in vices, jacks, presses, 3D printer Z axes and low-cost positioning stages.

Ball screw

In a ball screw, hardened steel balls roll in matching grooves in the screw and the nut, and are recirculated through a return tube or an internal deflector. The friction is rolling friction, so the efficiency is 90 % or more. Ball screws have almost no backlash (a preloaded double nut eliminates it completely), are very stiff, and can transmit high loads at high speed. They are the standard choice for CNC machine axes, industrial robot prismatic joints, precision positioning tables and aircraft actuators.

  • Advantages of a ball screw: efficiency above 90 % against 30 % to 60 % for a plain screw, so less heat and less motor power; very high positional accuracy and repeatability; zero backlash when preloaded; high load capacity and long life; smooth motion with no stick-slip; can be run at high speed.
  • Disadvantages: much more expensive; not self-locking, so a vertical axis needs a brake or a counterweight to hold the load when power is removed; needs precise support bearings (usually an angular contact pair at one end); sensitive to dirt, so a bellows or way cover is needed; the nut is bulky; if the screw is long and rotates fast, it can whip at its critical speed.
Formula

Lead screw relationsLinear travel per revolution = lead LLinear speed v = L × N / 60 (N in rpm, L in m)Resolution per motor step = L / (steps per revolution × microstepping factor)Torque to raise the load T = (F dm / 2) × (l + π μ dm secα) / (π dm − μ l secα)Efficiency η = tan λ / tan(λ + φ) where λ = lead angle and φ = friction angleSelf-locking condition: μ > tan λ (that is, the friction angle exceeds the lead angle)where dm is the mean diameter of the thread, l the lead, μ the coefficient of friction and α half the thread angle.

Example

Numerical 1. A ball screw of 10 mm lead is driven by a stepper motor of 1.8° step angle with 8 times microstepping. Steps per revolution = 200 × 8 = 1600. Resolution = 10/1600 = 0.00625 mm = 6.25 µm per step.
Numerical 2. The same screw must move a table at 0.5 m/s. Required speed = 0.5/0.010 = 50 revolutions per second = 3000 rpm.
Numerical 3. A plain lead screw of mean diameter 24 mm and lead 6 mm carries an axial load of 5 kN. The coefficient of friction is 0.12 and the thread is square. Lead angle λ = tan−1(6/(π×24)) = tan−1(0.0796) = 4.55°. Friction angle φ = tan−1(0.12) = 6.84°. Torque T = (F dm/2) tan(λ+φ) = (5000 × 0.024/2) × tan(11.39°) = 60 × 0.2014 = 12.1 N m. Efficiency = tan 4.55°/tan 11.39° = 0.0796/0.2014 = 39.5 %. Since φ > λ, the screw is self-locking.

Support and mounting of a screw

A screw must be supported by bearings. The three standard arrangements are: fixed-free (one end held by a bearing pair, the other end unsupported — cheap, but limited speed and accuracy, used for short screws), fixed-supported (one end held by an angular contact bearing pair and the other by a simple radial bearing — the usual arrangement), and fixed-fixed (both ends held by preloaded angular contact bearings — the stiffest and most accurate, allowing the highest speed, and permitting the screw to be pre-tensioned against thermal expansion). The nut is connected to the moving table through a nut housing, and the connection must be stiff or the table will lag.

2.Rack and Pinion

A rack is a straight bar with gear teeth, and a pinion is the small gear that meshes with it. Rotation of the pinion produces linear motion of the rack, or vice versa. This is the simplest and most direct way to convert rotary to linear motion over a long distance.

Linear travel per revolution = π × Dpinion = pc × Tpinion Linear speed v = ω r
  • Advantages: unlimited stroke length, limited only by the length of the rack; simple and robust; positive drive with no slip; high speed capability; can carry heavy loads; cheap to manufacture; the rack can be made in sections and joined end to end.
  • Disadvantages: backlash unless a spring-loaded pinion or a split rack is used; accuracy is limited by the manufacturing tolerance of the teeth and by the joint between rack sections; the gear mesh needs lubrication and protection from dust; the pinion must be accurately aligned with the rack; the linear resolution is coarser than a fine lead screw.
  • Applications: automobile steering (the most famous application — the steering wheel turns the pinion and the rack moves the track rods), lathe traverse and CNC gantry axes (a long X axis is much better done with a rack than with a 5 m screw, which would whip), sliding gates and doors, railway point machines, rack-and-pinion jacks, the boom of a crane, the height adjustment of a drill press or a milling machine, robot prismatic joints and linear actuators, and the focusing mechanism of a microscope.
Note

A rack and pinion is essentially a gear of infinite diameter. The teeth of the rack are straight-sided (an involute of infinite radius), and the same module must be used for both members.

3.Slider-Crank Mechanism

The slider-crank is the mechanism at the heart of the reciprocating engine and compressor. It has three moving links: the crank (which rotates fully about a fixed axis), the connecting rod (which has complex plane motion) and the slider (which translates along a fixed guide). There are three turning pairs and one sliding pair, and the mobility is 1.

Slider displacement x = r cos θ + √(l² − r² sin²θ) Stroke = 2r where n = l/r

For a long connecting rod (n large), the motion of the slider approaches simple harmonic motion. For a short rod, the motion becomes noticeably non-symmetrical: the slider moves faster during one half of the cycle than the other. This asymmetry is exploited in the quick-return mechanism.

  • In-line slider-crank — the slider path passes through the crank axis; forward and return strokes take equal time.
  • Offset slider-crank — the slider path is offset from the crank axis, so one stroke is quicker than the other. This is the basis of the quick-return action.
  • Inversions: fixing the crank gives the Whitworth quick-return mechanism (used in shaping machines); fixing the connecting rod gives the crank and slotted lever mechanism; fixing the slider gives the oscillating cylinder engine and hand pumps.
  • Applications: internal combustion engines (piston, connecting rod and crankshaft); reciprocating compressors and pumps; shaping, slotting and planing machines; presses and punch presses; the drive of some robot wrists and grippers; sewing machines; steam locomotives; windshield wiper mechanisms; and the Scotch yoke, which is a variant that produces exact simple harmonic motion.

4.Cams

Cams are described in detail in Topic 8. In the context of rotary-to-linear conversion in robots and automated machines, a cam is used when the linear motion must follow an exact, repeatable profile that is a function of the shaft angle, and when the speed is high. The follower's displacement is programmed into the shape of the cam, so no control system is needed at all — the program is in the metal.

  • Advantages in automation: extremely fast and repeatable; no controller, no sensor and no feedback needed; the motion profile is exactly as designed; several motions can be synchronised from one shaft; very reliable.
  • Disadvantages: the profile is fixed, so changing the motion means making a new cam; cams wear and are expensive to manufacture accurately; high contact stresses; need lubrication; not programmable.
  • Applications: valve gear of internal combustion engines; automatic lathes and screw machines; textile machinery; packaging and bottling machines; printing presses; indexing mechanisms; the feed of a punch press; and in older robots, where a cam on a motor shaft operated limit switches.
Note

In a modern robot, a cam is usually replaced in software by a motion profile or electronic cam: the controller generates a position-versus-time table that plays the same role as the cam profile, but it can be changed instantly by editing a number. This is one of the clearest illustrations of the mechatronic philosophy — hardware replaced by software.

5.Linkages and Coupler Curves

A linkage is an assembly of rigid links connected by joints that constrains the motion of the links in a useful way. The most important is the four-bar linkage, which has four links and four revolute joints, and mobility of one.

The coupler and the coupler curve

In a four-bar linkage the link that connects the crank to the rocker is called the coupler. The coupler has general plane motion — it both translates and rotates. Any point on the coupler (or on an extension of it) traces a closed curve called the coupler curve. These curves can be surprisingly complex and useful: they may have straight-line portions, cusps, loops and near-circular arcs.

  • Why coupler curves matter: by choosing the link lengths and the position of the coupler point, a designer can generate a wide variety of motion paths with a single rotating input and no control system at all.
  • Straight-line mechanisms: the Watt linkage, the Chebyshev lambda linkage and the Peaucellier-Lipkin linkage all produce exact or approximate straight-line motion from pure rotation, before the invention of the sliding pair. The Peaucellier cell gives an exact straight line using only revolute joints.
  • Roberts' theorem: any coupler curve can be generated by three different four-bar linkages, which gives the designer freedom to choose the most convenient arrangement.

Common linkages used in machines and robots

  • Crank-rocker — the crank rotates fully and the rocker oscillates. Used in wiper mechanisms, pump drives and the treadle of a sewing machine.
  • Double-crank (drag link) — both side links rotate fully. Used to couple two parallel shafts at a varying speed ratio.
  • Double-rocker — both side links oscillate.
  • Parallel linkage / pantograph — opposite links are equal and parallel, so the coupler translates without rotating. Used to keep a platform level, in scissor lifts, in the Delta robot, in the arms of some palletising robots, and in pantographs for copying and for electric locomotive current collectors.
  • Whitworth and crank-and-slotted-lever quick-return — used in shapers and slotters.
  • Toggle mechanism — near the fully extended position, a small input force produces a very large output force. Used in clamps, presses and locking devices.
  • Geneva mechanism — converts continuous rotation into intermittent indexing motion, used in film projectors, tool changers and indexing tables.
  • Five-bar and six-bar linkages — used where a four-bar cannot produce the required motion; two inputs are needed for a five-bar.
  • Leg mechanisms — the Theo Jansen linkage and the Klann linkage produce a walking motion from a single rotating crank, and are used in walking robots.

Linkages in robotics

Linkages are used in robots for three purposes: as the structure of parallel and Delta robots; as a transmission that keeps the motor on a fixed link (as in the closed-loop linkage of some SCARA and palletising robots); and as a motion generator for walking legs. A parallel manipulator such as the Stewart platform uses six linear actuators connecting a fixed base to a moving platform, giving six degrees of freedom with very high stiffness and accuracy — it is used in flight simulators, in machine tools and in precision positioning.

6.Couplings

Definition

A coupling is a device used to connect the ends of two shafts permanently or temporarily, in order to transmit torque. Couplings are needed wherever a motor drives a lead screw, a pinion or a gearbox.

Types of coupling

  • Rigid couplings — sleeve (muff), flanged and clamp types. Used only where the shafts are perfectly aligned, because any misalignment produces large bending loads on the bearings. Cheapest and stiffest.
  • Flexible couplings — tolerate some misalignment and absorb shock and vibration:
  •     • Oldham coupling — three discs, the middle one having two perpendicular tongues sliding in slots. Accommodates large parallel misalignment with zero backlash. Very common in 3D printers and light CNC axes.
  •     • Universal joint (Hooke's joint) — a cross piece between two yokes. Accommodates large angular misalignment up to about 30°, but the output speed fluctuates unless two joints are used in a correct arrangement (a double Cardan shaft).
  •     • Gear coupling — crowned teeth that slide, for heavy industrial drives.
  •     • Grid, chain and disc pack couplings — for heavy torque.
  •     • Elastomeric jaw (spider) coupling — two hubs with curved jaws and an elastomer spider between them. Cheap, damps vibration, and is fail-safe because the spider can be replaced. Has some backlash and wind-up.
  •     • Bellows coupling — a thin convoluted metal bellows welded between two hubs. Zero backlash, very high torsional stiffness, and excellent tolerance of misalignment. The standard choice for precision servo axes.
  •     • Beam / helical coupling — a single piece of aluminium with a helical slot cut in it. Zero backlash, inexpensive and maintenance free, widely used in small servo systems.
  • Fluid coupling and torque converter — transmit torque hydraulically, allowing slip and smooth starting; used in vehicle transmissions and large conveyor drives.
  • Safety (slip) coupling — disconnects or slips when the torque exceeds a set value, protecting the machine from a jam.

Selection of a coupling

  1. Torque to be transmitted, including the peak and shock torque, not just the continuous torque.
  2. Speed, and whether the assembly needs balancing above about 1800 rpm.
  3. Misalignment — parallel (offset), angular and axial (end float). Measure the actual misalignment; a good installation keeps it within the coupling's rating.
  4. Backlash requirement — precision positioning needs a zero-backlash coupling.
  5. Torsional stiffness — important for servo response; a soft coupling makes the servo loop oscillate.
  6. Space, inertia and cost — the coupling's own inertia adds to the motor's load.
  7. Environment — temperature, chemicals, lubrication and the need for electrical insulation between the shafts.
Important

Misalignment is the most common cause of premature failure in servo systems. Excess misalignment causes vibration, high bearing loads, coupling fatigue and, eventually, a broken shaft. Couplings tolerate misalignment but do not eliminate its effects. Always align motor and screw within the manufacturer's limits, usually a few hundredths of a millimetre and a few tenths of a degree.

★Summary

  • A lead screw converts rotation into linear motion; travel per revolution equals the lead. Acme and square threads are common.
  • A plain lead screw has 30 % to 60 % efficiency and is self-locking when the friction angle exceeds the lead angle.
  • A ball screw uses recirculating balls to give over 90 % efficiency, high accuracy, high stiffness and zero backlash, but it is costly and is not self-locking.
  • Efficiency of a power screw = tanλ/tan(λ+φ).
  • A rack and pinion gives unlimited stroke, is robust and cheap, but has backlash and lower accuracy; it is used in steering and in long machine axes.
  • The slider-crank converts rotation into reciprocating motion; x = r cosθ + √(l² − r²sin²θ) and the stroke is 2r. Offsetting it gives a quick-return action.
  • A cam gives an exact, fast, repeatable profile with no control system; in a robot it is replaced by a software motion profile (an electronic cam).
  • In a four-bar linkage the coupler has general plane motion and any point on it traces a coupler curve; these can contain useful straight-line portions.
  • Common linkages include the crank-rocker, the parallel linkage and pantograph, the toggle, the Geneva and the walking-leg linkages.
  • A coupling connects a motor to a screw or pinion. Bellows and beam couplings have zero backlash and are used in precision servo axes; Oldham couplings tolerate parallel misalignment; universal joints tolerate angular misalignment.
  • Misalignment is the commonest cause of servo system failure.
