1.The Manipulator as a Kinematic Chain

Definition

A manipulator is an open-loop kinematic chain of rigid links connected by joints, one end of which is fixed to the base and the other end of which carries the end effector. Because the chain is open, the position of the end effector depends on the angles (or extensions) of all the joints before it, and there is no closed loop of constraints.

Each joint provides one degree of freedom, so the number of joints equals the number of degrees of freedom of the arm. Six degrees of freedom are needed to place and orient a rigid body arbitrarily in space: three for position (x, y, z) and three for orientation (roll, pitch, yaw). A robot with exactly six axes can therefore reach any position and orientation within its workspace. A robot with more than six axes is called redundant; the extra freedom can be used to avoid obstacles, to avoid singularities or to keep the elbow away from the work. A robot with fewer than six axes cannot orient the tool arbitrarily, which is acceptable for tasks such as pick-and-place and palletising.

Terminology

  • Link — a rigid body between two joints. Links are numbered 0 (the base) to n (the last link carrying the tool).
  • Joint — the connection between two links. Revolute (R) joints rotate; prismatic (P) joints slide. These are lower pairs with one degree of freedom each.
  • Joint variable — the quantity that changes: θ for a revolute joint and d for a prismatic joint.
  • Arm — the first three joints, which position the wrist centre in space.
  • Wrist — the last three joints, which orient the tool. A spherical wrist has its three axes intersecting at one point, which decouples position from orientation and greatly simplifies the inverse kinematics.
  • Tool Centre Point (TCP) — the point on the tool whose position and orientation are controlled, for example the tip of a welding electrode or the centre of a gripper.
  • Base frame — the fixed coordinate system attached to the robot base.
  • Workspace — the volume that the TCP can reach.
  • Pose — the position and orientation of the TCP, described by six numbers.

Open and closed chains

Most industrial robots are open (serial) chains. Closed (parallel) chains connect the moving platform to the base by several independent limbs — the Delta robot and the Stewart platform are examples. Closed chains are much stiffer and more accurate because the errors of the limbs average out and because the motors can be mounted on the fixed base, but their workspace is small and their kinematics are far more complex. Hybrid (or tree) structures combine both, as in a dual-arm robot on a mobile base.

2.The Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) Convention

To calculate where the tool is, the position and orientation of every link must be described mathematically. The Denavit-Hartenberg convention, proposed in 1955, does this with just four numbers per link. A coordinate frame is attached to every link according to a fixed set of rules, and the transformation from one frame to the next is then completely described by four parameters.

Rules for assigning the frames

  1. The Z axis of frame i is placed along the axis of joint i+1 (the axis of motion of the joint).
  2. The origin of frame i is placed where the common normal between Zi-1 and Zi meets Zi. If the axes intersect, the origin is at the intersection. If they are parallel, the origin is chosen conveniently.
  3. The X axis of frame i lies along the common normal, pointing from Zi-1 towards Zi. If the axes intersect, X is chosen perpendicular to the plane containing both.
  4. The Y axis completes a right-handed coordinate system: Y = Z × X.
  5. Frame 0 is fixed to the base and frame n is fixed to the tool.

The four DH parameters

aiLink lengthDistance measured along Xi from Zi-1 to ZiNo — fixed by the geometry
αiLink twistAngle measured about Xi from Zi-1 to ZiNo — fixed by the geometry

The link transformation matrix

The transformation from frame i−1 to frame i is obtained by four simple motions performed in a fixed order: rotate about Z by θ, translate along Z by d, translate along X by a, and rotate about X by α.

Ai = Rot(z, θi) · Trans(z, di) · Trans(x, ai) · Rot(x, αi)

Written out in full, using c for cosine and s for sine:

Ai = [ cθ −sθ cα sθ sα a cθ ]
       [ sθ cθ cα −cθ sα a sθ ]
       [ 0 sα cα d ]
       [ 0 0 0 1 ]

The upper-left 3 × 3 block is the rotation matrix giving the orientation of the link; the right-hand 3 × 1 column is the position vector of the link origin. The last row is always [0 0 0 1], which is what makes the matrix homogeneous and allows rotation and translation to be combined in a single multiplication.

3.Forward and Inverse Kinematics

Forward kinematics

Forward (or direct) kinematics answers the question: given all the joint angles, where is the tool? The link transformations are simply multiplied together in order:

T0n = A1 A2 A3 … An

This is always straightforward and always has a unique answer. For a six-axis robot it involves multiplying six 4 × 4 matrices, which a modern controller does in microseconds. The result gives the position (x, y, z) of the TCP and its orientation, usually expressed as a rotation matrix, as Euler angles (roll, pitch, yaw) or as a quaternion.

Inverse kinematics

Inverse kinematics answers the opposite and much more important question: given the desired position and orientation of the tool, what joint angles are required? This is needed for Cartesian motion, for off-line programming from a CAD model, and for every vision-guided move.

  • It is much harder than the forward problem. The equations are non-linear, coupled and transcendental (they involve sines and cosines of sums of angles), so there is no general closed-form method.
  • There may be several solutions. A six-axis arm generally has up to eight distinct solutions for a given pose, corresponding to combinations of arm-up/arm-down, elbow-forward/elbow-backward and wrist-flip/wrist-no-flip. All eight reach the same tool pose but the arm takes a completely different shape.
  • There may be no solution, if the target is outside the workspace or if the required orientation cannot be achieved at that position.
  • Solution methods: closed-form (analytical) solutions exist for arms with special geometry, notably when three wrist axes intersect at a point (a spherical wrist), which is why almost all industrial robots are designed this way. Otherwise numerical iterative methods such as Newton-Raphson are used. Geometric methods solve the problem by trigonometry, link by link, and are the easiest to understand for a six-axis arm.
Note

Pieper's criterion: a closed-form solution exists for any six-revolute robot in which three consecutive axes intersect at a single point, or in which three consecutive axes are parallel. This is satisfied by virtually all industrial robots, which is why they can be programmed in Cartesian space.

Solution selection and continuity

When several solutions exist, the controller must choose one. It normally chooses the solution nearest to the current joint configuration, to minimise the movement. It must also stay on the same branch throughout a path, because switching branch in the middle of a move would cause a sudden large motion — a wrist flip or an elbow flip — which is dangerous. Robot languages allow the branch to be specified explicitly using configuration codes such as NUT/TURN, ELBOW UP/DOWN or W1/W2.

4.Workspace, Dexterity and Singularities

Workspace

The workspace is the volume that the TCP can reach. It is divided into the reachable workspace (all points the TCP can reach in at least one orientation) and the dexterous workspace (the smaller set of points that can be reached in every orientation). The shape depends on the configuration, on the link lengths and on the joint limits. It is normally specified as a set of cross-section drawings in the datasheet.

Dexterity and manipulability

Dexterity is a measure of how easily the robot can move the tool in arbitrary directions at a given point. It is high in the middle of the workspace and falls towards the edges. A common quantitative measure is the manipulability index, defined as the square root of the determinant of JJT:

w = √det( J JT )

This index is zero at a singularity and largest where the robot is most dexterous. It can be plotted as a map over the workspace and is used when siting a robot in a cell so that the working points lie in the region of highest dexterity.

Singularities

Definition

A singularity is a configuration in which the robot loses one or more degrees of freedom of tool motion. Mathematically, the Jacobian matrix J, which relates joint velocities to tool velocities (ẋ = Jθ̇), becomes rank deficient and its determinant becomes zero. The inverse J−1 does not exist, so a finite tool velocity would require an infinite joint velocity.

  • Shoulder (arm-extension) singularity — occurs when the wrist centre lies on the axis of joint 1, that is, directly above the base. Joint 1 rotation no longer moves the wrist centre, so one direction of motion is lost.
  • Elbow singularity — occurs when the arm is fully stretched out (elbow angle 180°) or fully folded. The tool can no longer move radially.
  • Wrist singularity — occurs in a spherical wrist when the axis of joint 4 becomes collinear with the axis of joint 6, which happens when joint 5 is at zero. Two joints then produce the same motion, so one degree of freedom is lost and the two joints can spin rapidly in opposite directions without moving the tool. This is the singularity most often seen in practice, and controllers limit the wrist speed to avoid it.
  • Boundary singularity — at the extreme edge of the workspace.
  • Internal singularity — in parallel robots, singularities can occur inside the workspace, which is one of their main disadvantages.

Consequences and remedies. Near a singularity the joint velocities demanded for a given Cartesian velocity become very large, the motors may saturate, the following error grows and the motion becomes jerky or stops with an error. Remedies are: avoid programming paths through singularities; offset the tool or the workpiece slightly so that the path misses them; reduce the Cartesian speed near a singularity; use a controller that automatically detects and slows down; or use a seven-axis redundant robot, which can pass through a singularity by moving the redundant joint.

The Jacobian

The Jacobian is the matrix of first partial derivatives that relates the rates of the joint variables to the linear and angular velocity of the tool:

ẋ = J(θ) θ̇ and for static forces τ = JT(θ) F

where ẋ is the 6 × 1 tool velocity vector (three linear and three angular components), θ̇ the n × 1 vector of joint velocities, τ the joint torque vector and F the force and torque applied at the tool. The Jacobian is therefore the bridge between joint space and Cartesian space, and it is used for Cartesian velocity control, for force control, for manipulability analysis and for singularity detection. It is configuration dependent, so it must be recomputed continuously during motion.

5.Worked Example — Forward Kinematics of a Two-Link Planar Arm

This is the simplest complete example and shows exactly how the method works. Consider a planar arm with two revolute joints, link lengths L1 and L2, and joint angles θ1 and θ2.

The position of the elbow and of the tool tip is found by simple trigonometry:

x1 = L1 cos θ1 , y1 = L1 sin θ1
x = L1 cos θ1 + L2 cos(θ1 + θ2)
y = L1 sin θ1 + L2 sin(θ1 + θ2)

The inverse problem is solved as follows. From the two equations,

x² + y² = L1² + L2² + 2 L1 L2 cos θ2cos θ2 = (x² + y² − L1² − L2²) / (2 L1 L2) and θ2 = ± cos−1( ... )

The two signs of θ2 are the elbow-up and elbow-down solutions. Once θ2 is known, θ1 follows from

θ1 = tan−1(y/x) − tan−1[ L2 sin θ2 / (L1 + L2 cos θ2) ]
Example

Numerical. Let L1 = 0.4 m and L2 = 0.3 m, and let the required tool position be x = 0.5 m, y = 0.3 m. Then x² + y² = 0.25 + 0.09 = 0.34. cos θ2 = (0.34 − 0.16 − 0.09)/(2 × 0.4 × 0.3) = 0.09/0.24 = 0.375, so θ2 = 67.98° (elbow down) or −67.98° (elbow up). For the elbow-down solution, sin θ2 = 0.927, so θ1 = tan−1(0.3/0.5) − tan−1[(0.3 × 0.927)/(0.4 + 0.3 × 0.375)] = 30.96° − tan−1(0.2781/0.5125) = 30.96° − 28.44° = 2.52°. Checking: x = 0.4 cos 2.52° + 0.3 cos 70.5° = 0.3996 + 0.1003 = 0.500 m ✓ and y = 0.4 sin 2.52° + 0.3 sin 70.5° = 0.0176 + 0.2825 = 0.300 m ✓.

Note

Notice that a solution exists only if the target is within the workspace, that is, if L1 − L2 ≤ r ≤ L1 + L2 where r = √(x²+y²). Here r = 0.583 m, which lies between 0.1 m and 0.7 m, so the point is reachable. If r were greater than 0.7 m the value of cos θ2 would exceed 1 and no solution would exist.

★Summary

  • A manipulator is an open kinematic chain of rigid links joined by revolute or prismatic joints, with the base fixed and the end effector at the far end.
  • Six degrees of freedom are needed to position and orient a rigid body arbitrarily; more than six makes the robot redundant.
  • The first three joints (the arm) position the wrist centre and the last three (the wrist) orient the tool.
  • The Denavit-Hartenberg convention describes each link with four parameters: θ (joint angle), d (link offset), a (link length) and α (link twist). θ is the variable for a revolute joint and d for a prismatic joint.
  • Each link is described by a 4 × 4 homogeneous transformation matrix Ai = Rot(z,θ) Trans(z,d) Trans(x,a) Rot(x,α).
  • Forward kinematics multiplies the matrices: T = A1A2…An. It is simple and unique.
  • Inverse kinematics is non-linear and may have up to eight solutions or none; closed-form solutions exist when three consecutive axes intersect (Pieper's criterion).
  • The controller must select a solution branch and must not switch branch during a move.
  • The workspace is divided into the reachable workspace and the smaller dexterous workspace.
  • A singularity is a configuration in which the Jacobian becomes rank deficient and a degree of freedom is lost; the shoulder, elbow and wrist singularities are the three common types.
  • The Jacobian relates joint velocities to tool velocities (ẋ = Jθ̇) and tool forces to joint torques (τ = JTF).
  • Serial robots have a large workspace but low stiffness; parallel robots are stiffer and faster but have a small workspace.
