14Basic Elements of an Open Loop System

A detailed look at each element of an open loop control system, with worked examples such as a toaster, a traffic light, a stepper motor drive and an automatic washing machine timer.

On this page

  1. Structure of an Open Loop System

1.Structure of an Open Loop System

An open loop control system has a signal path that goes in one direction only, from the input to the output. There is no measuring element and no comparison point. The system assumes that the output will be whatever the input commanded, and it has no way of knowing whether that actually happened.

The elements are as follows.

  1. Input / command / reference — the desired output expressed in terms the controller can understand. It may be a dial setting, a keypad entry, a switch closure, a pulse train or a stored program. The input is usually low power, so it needs amplification.
  2. Controller — the decision-making element. It interprets the input and produces a command for the actuator. It may be a cam-and-lever mechanism, a timer, an electronic circuit, a microcontroller running a fixed program, or a PLC executing a sequence.
  3. Actuator / final control element — the device that actually applies energy to the plant: a heater element, a motor, a solenoid valve, a lamp, a relay or a contactor.
  4. Plant / process — the physical system being controlled: the bread in a toaster, the traffic at a junction, the water in a tank, the table of a machine.
  5. Output / controlled variable — the result: the colour of the toast, the state of the traffic lights, the position of the table.
  6. Power supply and interconnections — the energy source for the actuator and the wiring that carries the command signals.
Note

The defining feature is the absence of a feedback path. If the output is disturbed, the system continues exactly as before and the output simply drifts away from the desired value.

2.Worked Examples of Open Loop Systems

Example 1 — Electric toaster

The user sets a browning dial which adjusts the length of time for which the heater is energised. The controller is a bimetallic strip and a spring mechanism (or an electronic timer). The actuator is the nichrome heating element. The plant is the slice of bread. The output is the toasted bread.

There is no temperature sensor looking at the bread. If the bread is cold, thick or damp, or if the mains voltage is low, or if several slices are toasted one after another so that the toaster is already hot, the result will be different from what the dial setting implies. The system cannot detect or correct this. That is why the first slice from a cold toaster is often lighter than the second.

Example 2 — Traffic light with fixed timing

A timer circuit or a PLC program switches the lights in a fixed sequence with fixed durations. The input is the timing program, the controller is the timer, the actuator is the relay driving the lamps, the plant is the traffic junction and the output is the light colour. The system does not know how many vehicles are waiting. During a traffic jam the green time may be too short, and during the night the lights may be red when no one is there.

Example 3 — Stepper motor position control

A microcontroller sends N pulses to a stepper motor driver, expecting the shaft to turn by N times the step angle. No encoder is fitted. This works very well as long as the motor is never overloaded, never accelerates too fast and never hits resonance, because then it would lose steps and the actual position would differ from the calculated one — and nobody would know.

Example 4 — Automatic washing machine on a timed cycle

The programmer advances through fill, wash, rinse and spin on the basis of time alone. The water level may be checked by a switch (which is a partial feedback), but the cleanliness of the clothes is never measured, so the cycle always takes the same time however dirty the load is.

Example 5 — Others

Immersion water heater on a timer; automatic lawn sprinkler on a timer; a DC motor run from a fixed-voltage supply; a printing press feeding paper at a set rate; a microwave oven with a time setting; an assembly robot that moves to fixed taught positions without checking; a music system volume control; a bench drill press.

3.Advantages, Limitations and When to Use an Open Loop System

  • Advantages: simple in construction and easy to design; low cost because there is no sensor, no comparator and no complex controller; easy to maintain and to troubleshoot; inherently stable — there is no feedback, so it cannot oscillate; convenient when the output is difficult, dangerous or expensive to measure; gives good results when the input-output relationship is well known, repeatable and the disturbance is negligible.
  • Limitations: inaccurate; the output cannot be corrected when a disturbance occurs or when the plant parameters change with ageing, temperature or wear; periodic recalibration is needed; requires an accurately known and stable relationship between input and output; performance varies with the load; cannot adapt to changing conditions; a fault may go undetected, which can be dangerous.

Despite the limitations, open loop control is used very widely because for many applications the accuracy obtained is perfectly adequate and the cost saving is large. Good engineering means using the simplest arrangement that meets the specification, not always adding feedback.

A common and effective compromise is calibrated open loop control: the system is open loop during operation, but a sensor is used occasionally to recalibrate it. For example, a 3D printer with a stepper motor is open loop, but it has limit switches that are used at start-up to establish a known zero position, removing the accumulated error.

Example

Choosing between the two. Consider controlling the water flow through a pipe. If the pump runs at a constant speed and the downstream pressure is constant, the flow is essentially constant and an open loop system is adequate and cheap. If the downstream pressure varies (for example other taps are opened), the flow will vary too and a closed loop system with a flow meter becomes necessary. The decision depends entirely on whether the disturbances are significant compared with the accuracy required.

★Summary

  • An open loop system has a one-way signal path with no measuring element and no comparison point.
  • Its basic elements are the input/command, the controller, the actuator (final control element), the plant or process, the output, and the power supply.
  • The output is never measured, so any disturbance or any change in plant parameters produces an error that cannot be corrected.
  • Advantages are simplicity, low cost, easy maintenance and guaranteed stability.
  • Limitations are inaccuracy, the need for periodic calibration, sensitivity to load and parameter changes, and undetected faults.
  • Typical examples are a toaster, a fixed-time traffic light, a stepper motor drive without an encoder, a sprinkler on a timer and a manually set clothes iron.
  • Open loop control is appropriate when the input-output relationship is well known, stable and repeatable, and when the required accuracy is modest.
  • Calibrated open loop control, such as homing a stepper-driven machine against limit switches at start-up, is a useful compromise.
