03LVDT, Eddy Current Proximity Sensor and Hall Effect Sensor

Three important displacement and position sensors: the linear variable differential transformer for accurate linear displacement, the eddy current probe for non-contact metal detection, and the Hall effect sensor for magnetic field and speed measurement.

On this page

  1. Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT)
  2. Eddy Current Proximity Sensor
  3. Hall Effect Sensor
  4. Comparison of the Three Sensors

1.Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT)

Construction

LVDT stands for Linear Variable Differential Transformer. It is an inductive transducer that converts linear displacement into an alternating voltage. Its construction is very simple and robust. A hollow cylindrical former, made of a non-magnetic material such as glass-reinforced plastic or ceramic, carries three windings. A single primary winding P is placed at the centre, and two identical secondary windings S1 and S2 are wound on either side of it. The two secondaries are connected in series opposition, meaning that their voltages oppose each other.

A separate soft iron core, usually made of nickel-iron alloy, slides freely inside the hollow former. The core is attached to the object whose displacement is to be measured. There is no mechanical contact between the core and the windings, which is a great advantage because there is no friction and no wear.

Working principle

An alternating voltage, typically 1 V to 24 V at 50 Hz to 20 kHz, is applied to the primary winding. This produces an alternating magnetic flux inside the former, which links with both secondary windings and induces voltages V1 in S1 and V2 in S2.

  • Null or central position. When the core is exactly at the centre, the flux linking both secondaries is equal, so V1 = V2. Because the windings are in opposition, the net output Vout = V1 − V2 = 0. This is called the null position.
  • Core moved to the left. More flux links S1 than S2, so V1 > V2 and the output is a positive alternating voltage.
  • Core moved to the right. More flux links S2, so V2 > V1 and the output is an alternating voltage of the same magnitude but reversed in phase by 180°.

Therefore the magnitude of the output voltage tells us how far the core has moved and the phase tells us in which direction. This is why the word differential appears in the name.

Formula

Output of an LVDTVout = V1 − V2 = S × xwhere S is the sensitivity in mV/mm and x is the displacement of the core from the null position.

Signal conditioning

Because the output is an alternating voltage, it cannot be read directly by a microcontroller. A demodulator circuit is used: the AC output is first rectified, then filtered and finally converted into a DC voltage whose polarity indicates direction. Many commercial LVDT modules include this conditioning, or a dedicated IC such as the AD598 or AD698 is used.

Example

Numerical. An LVDT has a sensitivity of 2.5 mV/mm and is supplied from a 3 V, 5 kHz source. The core is displaced by 8 mm to the right. Find the output voltage. Solution: Vout = S × x = 2.5 × 8 = 20 mV, and because the movement is to the right its phase is reversed by 180° with respect to the left-side reference.

2.Eddy Current Proximity Sensor

Principle

The eddy current sensor works on Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction and Lenz's law. When a coil carrying a high-frequency alternating current is brought near a conducting object, the alternating magnetic field of the coil cuts the conductor and induces circulating currents inside it. These swirling currents are called eddy currents. By Lenz's law, the eddy currents create their own magnetic field which opposes the original field of the coil. This opposition reduces the effective inductance of the coil and increases its effective resistance, so the impedance of the coil changes. The change in impedance depends on the distance to the target, and this is what is measured.

Construction

The sensing head contains a small coil wound on a ferrite core, housed in a stainless steel barrel with an active face. A built-in oscillator produces a high frequency, typically 100 Hz to 1 MHz (most industrial sensors use around 1 kHz to 20 kHz), and feeds it to the coil. A detector or trigger circuit monitors the amplitude of oscillation. When a metal target approaches, the oscillation amplitude falls (or the coil impedance changes) and a Schmitt trigger produces a clean digital output. Many versions also give an analogue output proportional to distance.

Working

  1. The oscillator drives an alternating current through the coil, creating an alternating magnetic field in front of the sensing face.
  2. A conductive target enters this field and eddy currents are induced on its surface.
  3. The eddy currents produce an opposing field which loads the coil; energy is drawn from the oscillator.
  4. The amplitude of oscillation decreases. The detector senses this decrease.
  5. When the target comes within the sensing distance, the trigger flips and the output changes state.
  6. When the target moves away, the oscillation recovers and the output returns to its original state.

Important terms

  • Sensing distance (Sn) — the maximum distance at which a standard mild steel target is detected, usually 1 mm to 40 mm.
  • Hysteresis — the difference between the operate and release distances, typically 5 % to 20 % of Sn. It prevents the output from chattering when the target is exactly at the boundary.
  • Repeat accuracy — how consistently the sensor switches at the same distance, typically 1 % to 3 % of Sn.
  • Response frequency — how many switching cycles per second the sensor can perform, typically 200 Hz to 5 kHz.
  • Output configuration — PNP (sources current) or NPN (sinks current), normally open or normally closed, two-wire, three-wire or four-wire.
Note

The response of an eddy current sensor depends strongly on the material of the target. Ferrous metals (iron, steel) give the full rated distance. Non-ferrous metals such as aluminium, copper and brass give a much shorter distance, often only 40 % to 70 % of the rated value, and a reduction factor is published by the manufacturer. Non-conductors such as plastic, wood and glass are not detected at all.

Advantages, disadvantages and applications

  • Advantages: completely non-contact, no wear; unaffected by dust, oil, water, dirt and light; very high switching frequency; can measure small displacements with high resolution; works through non-metallic barriers.
  • Disadvantages: detects metals only; sensing distance is short; output depends on the target material, size and shape; affected by strong external magnetic fields and by temperature.
  • Applications: counting and detecting metal parts on a conveyor; measuring shaft vibration and axial displacement in turbines; checking runout of a rotating shaft; metal detection in food and pharmaceutical packaging; measuring thickness of metal sheets; position feedback in precision machine tools; non-destructive crack testing.
Example

An eddy current proximity sensor has a rated sensing distance of 8 mm for mild steel and a reduction factor of 0.4 for aluminium. The largest distance at which it will detect an aluminium target is 8 × 0.4 = 3.2 mm.

3.Hall Effect Sensor

The Hall effect

The Hall effect was discovered by Edwin Hall in 1879. When a current-carrying conductor or semiconductor is placed in a magnetic field that is perpendicular to the direction of current, the charge carriers experience a force and are pushed to one side of the material. This causes a build-up of charge on the two opposite faces and produces a small voltage across the material, perpendicular to both the current and the field. This voltage is called the Hall voltage.

Formula

Hall voltageVH = (B × I) / (n × q × t)where B = flux density (tesla), I = control current (A), n = charge carrier density, q = electron charge, t = thickness of the slab. Since the quantity 1/(nqt) is constant for a given material, we write VH = KH × B × I, so the Hall voltage is directly proportional to both the field strength and the control current.

Metals have a very large carrier density n, so their Hall voltage is extremely small. Semiconductors such as gallium arsenide (GaAs), indium antimonide (InSb), indium arsenide (InAs) and silicon have a much smaller n and therefore give a much larger Hall voltage. This is why Hall elements are made from semiconductor materials and are very thin.

Types of Hall sensor

  • Linear (analogue) Hall sensor — the output voltage is directly proportional to the magnetic flux density. Used where the strength of the field must be measured, for example in a current clamp meter or a brushless DC motor for commutation. Example: A1302, SS49E.
  • Digital (switching) Hall sensor — contains a Schmitt trigger, so the output is either HIGH or LOW. It switches when the field exceeds a certain operate point and releases when it falls below a certain release point. Used for position, speed and limit sensing. Example: A3144, US1881.
  • Latching Hall sensor — switches ON with one magnetic polarity (south) and OFF with the opposite polarity (north). Ideal for brushless motor commutation and for detecting the poles of a rotating ring magnet.

Working and interfacing

In a digital Hall switch, the Hall element output is amplified and fed to a Schmitt trigger. The output stage is usually an open-collector NPN transistor, so an external pull-up resistor (typically 1 kΩ to 10 kΩ) must be connected between the output pin and the positive supply. When a south pole of a magnet comes close, the output transistor saturates and the pin goes LOW; when the magnet is removed, the output goes HIGH through the pull-up. This gives a clean square wave that a microcontroller can count.

Speed measurement using a Hall sensor

This is the most common industrial application. A small magnet is fixed on the rotating shaft or a toothed ferrous wheel is used with a Hall switch placed near it. Each time a magnet or tooth passes, one pulse is produced. If N pulses are counted in T seconds and the wheel has P teeth,

Speed in revolutions per second = N / (P × T) and Speed in rpm = 60 × N / (P × T)
Example

Numerical. A shaft carries a disc with 20 teeth and a Hall sensor produces 1500 pulses in 10 seconds. Speed = 60 × 1500 / (20 × 10) = 90000 / 200 = 450 rpm.

Advantages, disadvantages and applications

  • Advantages: solid state, so no contacts, no wear and no bouncing; works at very high speed; immune to dust, dirt, water, oil and vibration; small size and low cost; can sense through non-magnetic walls; provides a digital output directly.
  • Disadvantages: requires a magnet or a magnetic target, so it cannot sense non-magnetic materials directly; affected by stray magnetic fields and by high temperature; the sensing gap is limited to a few millimetres; needs a pull-up resistor.
  • Applications: speed and position sensing in brushless DC motors (for commutation); wheel speed sensing in ABS and in tachometers; crankshaft and camshaft position in automobiles; detecting the position of a pneumatic cylinder piston through a non-magnetic barrel; brushless DC fan and pump control; current sensing (the current-carrying conductor produces a field which the Hall element measures); joysticks and throttle pedals; proximity switches, door switches and level indicators with a floating magnet.

★Summary

  • An LVDT has one primary and two secondaries connected in series opposition, with a sliding soft iron core. Output magnitude gives displacement and phase gives direction; output is zero at the null position.
  • LVDT advantages are infinite resolution, no friction, long life and excellent linearity; its drawback is that the output is AC and needs demodulation.
  • An eddy current sensor uses a high-frequency coil. Eddy currents induced in a nearby conductor oppose the coil field and change its impedance, which is detected as a change in oscillation amplitude.
  • Eddy current sensors detect metals only, are non-contact and immune to contamination, and their sensing distance depends on the target material through a reduction factor.
  • The Hall effect produces a voltage VH = KH B I across a thin current-carrying semiconductor placed in a perpendicular magnetic field.
  • Hall sensors are available as linear (analogue), digital switching and latching types; digital types need an external pull-up resistor.
  • Speed from a Hall sensor: rpm = 60 N / (P T), where N is the number of pulses, P the number of teeth or poles and T the time in seconds.
