17Examples of Mechatronic Systems — Room Heating, Water Level Control and Washing Machine

Three complete case studies that bring together everything in Units I and II: a closed loop room heating system, an automatic water level controller, and a sequential washing machine system.

1.Case Study 1 — Heating a Room (Closed Loop System)

This is the simplest and clearest example of closed loop control and is worth understanding thoroughly, because every other control system follows the same pattern.

Requirement

The air temperature of a room must be maintained at a value chosen by the user, say 24 °C, even though the outside temperature changes through the day, doors and windows are opened, and people and appliances add heat.

The elements

Table 17.1 — Elements of the room heating system
ElementDevice usedFunction
Set pointThermostat dial or digital keypadStores the desired temperature
Error detectorComparison inside the thermostatFinds the difference between desired and measured temperature
ControllerBimetal switch, electronic comparator or microcontroller with PIDDecides when to switch the heater
ActuatorContactor / relay / solid state relaySwitches the heater current
Final control elementResistance heater, heat pump or hot water valveAdds heat to the room
PlantThe room (air plus walls and furniture)Stores and loses heat
Measurement elementBimetal strip, thermistor, RTD, LM35 or digital sensor such as the DHT11Measures the actual room temperature
DisturbanceOutside temperature, open doors and windows, sunlight, people, appliancesPushes the temperature away from the set point

Working

The temperature sensor produces a signal proportional to the room temperature. The controller compares it with the set point. While the room temperature is below the set point, the error is positive and the heater is ON. Heat is added, the room warms up, and the error reduces. When the temperature reaches the set point, the heater is switched OFF. The room now loses heat through the walls and windows, so the temperature falls again. When it falls below the set point by a small amount, the heater is switched ON again. The cycle repeats and the temperature is held close to the set point.

On-off control and hysteresis

The simplest controller is on-off (also called bang-bang or two-position) control: the heater is either fully on or fully off. If the switching were exactly at the set point, the heater would chatter on and off many times a second because of the sensor's own noise and the tiny fluctuations of air temperature. This would destroy the contacts and be very inefficient.

To prevent this, a hysteresis band (also called the differential or the dead band) is introduced. The heater switches OFF at, say, 24.5 °C and switches ON again only at 23.5 °C. The temperature therefore oscillates between these two limits, and the switching rate is reduced to a few times per hour.

A bimetallic thermostat achieves this naturally: a strip of two metals with different expansion coefficients is bonded together and bends when heated. It carries the contacts and is placed near a small heater, so its own temperature rises a little when the contacts close, which automatically creates the hysteresis.

Better control and specifications

  • Proportional control — the heater power is varied continuously in proportion to the error, using phase-controlled thyristors or PWM. The temperature settles much closer to the set point with far less oscillation, but a steady-state offset remains.
  • PID control — proportional plus integral plus derivative action removes the offset and gives a fast, smooth response. Used in industrial ovens, incubators and precision environmental chambers.
  • Fuzzy logic and adaptive control — used in modern air conditioners to take account of room size, occupancy and outside conditions, improving comfort and saving energy.

Typical performance specifications of a room heater controller are: set point range 5 °C to 35 °C, accuracy ±1 °C, hysteresis 0.5 °C to 2 °C (adjustable), switching rating 16 A at 230 V AC, sensor type NTC thermistor of 10 kΩ at 25 °C, and supply 230 V AC.

Note

A closely related system is the refrigerator or air conditioner, which is exactly the same closed loop with the actuator removing heat instead of adding it. The human body is also a closed loop temperature control system, using temperature sensors in the skin and hypothalamus, with sweating and shivering as the actuators.

2.Case Study 2 — Automatic Control of Water Level

Requirement

An overhead tank must be kept filled from an underground sump by a motor pump. The pump must start automatically when the tank level falls, must stop when the tank is full, must not overflow, must not run when the sump is empty (which would damage it), and must not require anyone to climb up and switch it on.

The elements

Table 17.2 — Elements of the water level control system
ElementDeviceFunction
Set pointPositions of the LOW and HIGH probes (or the float switches)Defines the level band within which the pump operates
Measurement elementConductivity probes, float and reed switches, or an ultrasonic sensorDetects the water level
ControllerRelay logic circuit, dedicated water level controller IC, or a microcontrollerImplements the start/stop logic and the interlocks
Actuator / final control elementContactor and single-phase or three-phase induction motor driving a centrifugal pumpDelivers water from the sump to the tank
PlantThe overhead tankStores the water
Controlled outputWater level in the tankThe variable being controlled
DisturbanceWater being drawn off by users, evaporation, leaks, and sump level changesChanges the level

Working

  1. Two stainless steel electrodes (probes) are suspended in the overhead tank: one near the bottom (LOW) and one near the top (HIGH). A third common electrode is placed at the bottom, and a fourth is placed in the sump.
  2. A small low-voltage AC signal (usually 9 V to 12 V AC, never DC, because DC causes electrolysis and corrodes the probes) is applied. When water covers a probe, current flows through the water and the probe circuit is completed.
  3. When the level falls below the LOW probe, the LOW circuit opens. The controller detects this and energises the pump contactor, provided the sump probe also shows that water is available.
  4. The pump runs and the level rises. As soon as water covers the LOW probe again, the controller latches itself so that the pump keeps running even though the LOW condition has been satisfied.
  5. When the water reaches the HIGH probe, the controller de-energises the contactor and the pump stops. The tank is now full.
  6. If at any time the sump probe indicates that the sump is empty, the pump is stopped immediately to prevent dry running, which would destroy the pump seal and burn the motor.
  7. An alarm may be sounded if the tank overflows (a separate overflow probe above the HIGH probe) or if the sump has been empty for a long time.

The logic

PUMP ON = (LOW probe dry) AND (SUMP has water) AND (HIGH probe dry) AND (not overloaded)
PUMP OFF = (HIGH probe wet) OR (SUMP dry) OR (overload trip) OR (E-stop)

This logic is easily implemented with two relays forming a latching circuit, with a dedicated controller IC, or with a microcontroller. The latching action gives the system hysteresis, exactly like the room heater: the pump does not chatter as the water surface waves near a single probe.

Other methods of level control

  • Float switches — a float with a reed switch at each level. Robust, simple and cheap; the most common method in domestic installations. See Topic 4.
  • Ultrasonic level sensor — non-contact, measures the level continuously rather than at discrete points, so proportional control is possible. Ideal for corrosive or dirty liquids.
  • Capacitive probe — works with non-conducting liquids such as diesel and oils, where conductivity probes fail.
  • Hydrostatic pressure sensor — a submersible pressure transducer at the bottom of the tank; pressure is proportional to the height of liquid.
  • Displacer / servo level gauge — used in refineries for accurate interface level measurement.
  • Load cell under the tank — gives the weight, hence the quantity, of the contents.
  • Radar / guided wave radar — non-contact, unaffected by vapour, dust or temperature; used in large industrial tanks.

Applications and specifications

Automatic water level controllers are used in domestic overhead tank systems, in industrial water storage and treatment plants, in boiler feed water control (a critical safety application, since a boiler running dry can explode), in cooling tower make-up, in chemical dosing tanks, in sewage and drainage pump stations, in swimming pools, in agricultural irrigation sumps and in fire fighting water storage.

Typical specifications of a domestic controller are: supply 230 V AC 50 Hz, probe voltage 9 V to 12 V AC, contact rating 5 A to 16 A, number of levels 2 to 4, adjustable sensitivity, LED indication of each level, dry-run protection, overload protection through the pump starter's thermal relay, and enclosure rating IP54 or better.

Important

Never use DC for conductivity probes and never let the probe voltage exceed the safe extra-low voltage limit (about 25 V AC). Probes corrode over time and must be inspected periodically. The pump must always be protected by a thermal overload relay and a dry-run interlock.

3.Case Study 3 — Washing Machine System

Requirement

A domestic fully automatic washing machine must wash a load of clothes through a programmed sequence of filling, heating, washing, draining, rinsing and spinning, with the correct amount of water at the correct temperature, safely, without any attention from the user.

This system is interesting because it combines sequential control (the cycle), closed loop control (the water level and the water temperature) and open loop control (the wash timer) all in one machine.

The elements

Table 17.3 — Elements of a washing machine
SubsystemSensorsControllerActuators
Water fillingWater level pressure switch or float switchMicrocontrollerInlet solenoid valve (one or two, for hot and cold)
Water heatingNTC thermistor or thermostatMicrocontroller with on-off or PID controlImmersion heater with thermal cut-out
WashingMotor tachometer (in some models)MicrocontrollerWash motor with belt or direct drive, driving the drum alternately clockwise and anticlockwise
DrainingEmpty-level switch, sometimes a turbidity sensorMicrocontrollerDrain pump and drain valve
SpinningMotor speed feedback, out-of-balance sensorMicrocontroller ramps the speed up graduallyMotor at high speed (800 to 1400 rpm)
SafetyDoor lock switch, thermal cut-out, overflow switchMicrocontroller inhibits all action if unsafeDoor lock solenoid, buzzer, drain pump
User interfaceProgram selector, keypad, sometimes a load weight sensorMicrocontroller stores the programsLCD or LED display, buzzer

Working of a typical cycle

  1. Door lock. The user closes the door and presses start. The controller first energises the door lock solenoid, which mechanically locks the door and, through a switch, confirms to the controller that the door is locked. No other action can start until this confirmation is received.
  2. Water filling. The inlet solenoid valve opens and water enters the tub. The level is measured by a pressure switch: as the water rises, air trapped in a small tube connected to the bottom of the tub is compressed, and when the pressure reaches a set value the switch operates. Alternatively a float switch is used. The valve closes when the set level is reached. This is a closed loop control of level.
  3. Heating. If a warm wash is selected, the immersion heater is switched on and the thermistor is monitored until the set temperature is reached, when the heater is switched off. A separate thermal cut-out is a safety device that operates if the controller fails. This is another closed loop control.
  4. Washing. The motor turns the drum slowly (about 50 rpm) alternately clockwise for a few seconds, pauses, then anticlockwise. This agitation, together with the detergent, loosens the dirt. The duration is fixed by the program, so this part is open loop (timed). Some advanced machines have a turbidity sensor that measures how dirty the water is, making it closed loop.
  5. Draining. The drain pump runs until the empty-level switch indicates that the tub is empty.
  6. Rinsing. Clean water is filled, the drum is agitated again, and the water is drained. The number of rinses depends on the program.
  7. Spinning. The motor accelerates the drum gradually to 800 rpm to 1400 rpm to extract water by centrifugal force. The acceleration is ramped slowly, and if an out-of-balance condition is detected (from the motor current or a dedicated sensor), the controller slows down, redistributes the clothes by rocking the drum, and tries again. This protects the bearings and the suspension.
  8. End. The buzzer sounds, the door lock is released after a short delay (so that the drum has fully stopped), and the machine returns to the idle state.

Why this is a good mechatronic example

  • It uses every key element: sensors (level, temperature, door, speed, out-of-balance), signal conditioning, a digital controller with a stored program, several actuators (valve, heater, motor, pump, door lock solenoid), and a mechanical plant (drum, tub, suspension, belt).
  • It shows the combination of sequential control for the cycle and closed loop control for level and temperature.
  • It shows how software provides the flexibility: the mechanical hardware is identical for all models, but different programs, different spin speeds and different features are simply different software.
  • It shows the importance of safety interlocks: the machine physically cannot spin with the door unlocked or with water in the tub.
  • Modern machines add fuzzy logic to decide the water quantity from the weight and absorbency of the clothes, inverter drives with brushless motors for quiet and efficient spinning, auto-dosing of detergent, and Wi-Fi connectivity for remote monitoring.

Other similar mechatronic case studies

The same reasoning can be applied to a dishwasher (fill, heat, spray, drain, dry), a microwave oven (timer plus humidity or temperature sensor plus magnetron power control), an automatic car park barrier, a vending machine, a photocopier, an elevator, a CNC machine and an industrial robot. Each one is a combination of sensors, a controller and actuators working together through a mechanical structure — which is exactly what mechatronics means.

★Summary

  • Room heating is a closed loop system: set point, thermostat comparator, contactor, heater, room as the plant, and a temperature sensor as the measurement element.
  • On-off control with a hysteresis band is the simplest form; the band prevents contact chatter. Proportional and PID control give a closer and smoother regulation.
  • A bimetal strip naturally provides hysteresis because its own heater warms it when the contacts close.
  • Automatic water level control uses two or more probes or float switches in the tank plus a sump probe, a relay or microcontroller, and a pump contactor.
  • Conductivity probes must be driven with low-voltage AC, never DC, to avoid electrolytic corrosion.
  • The pump logic is latched, giving hysteresis, and includes a dry-run interlock and an overload trip.
  • A washing machine combines sequential control of the cycle with closed loop control of water level and temperature, and open loop timed control of the wash period.
  • Its safety interlocks ensure that the door must be locked before any action and that the drum cannot spin with water in the tub.
  • In all three systems the intelligence lies in the controller and the software, while the mechanical hardware stays simple.
