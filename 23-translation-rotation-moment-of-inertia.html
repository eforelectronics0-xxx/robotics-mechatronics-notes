23Elementary Mechanical Concepts — Translation, Rotation and Moment of Inertia

The mechanics an engineer must know before designing a robot arm: linear and rotational motion, the equations that govern them, and above all moment of inertia, which decides how much torque a joint motor must produce.

  2. Rotational Motion

1.Translation (Linear Motion)

Definition

Translation or linear motion is the motion of a body in which every particle of the body moves through the same distance, in the same direction, in the same interval of time. All points of the body have the same velocity and the same acceleration.

Translation may be rectilinear (along a straight line, such as the piston of a cylinder or the table of a 3D printer) or curvilinear (along a curved path, with all points keeping the same orientation, such as the platform of a parallel linkage). In robot design, linear motion appears in prismatic joints, in lead screw drives and in gantry axes.

Equations of linear motion

  • Velocity v = ds/dt, measured in m/s.
  • Acceleration a = dv/dt, measured in m/s².
  • Momentum p = m v, measured in kg m/s.
  • Force F = m a (Newton's second law), measured in newtons.
  • Kinetic energy KE = ½ m v², measured in joules.
  • Work W = F s, and power P = F v, measured in watts.
Formula

Equations of uniformly accelerated linear motionv = u + a ts = u t + ½ a t²v² = u² + 2 a swhere u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, s = distance and t = time.

Example

A robot gantry axis must move 0.6 m in 1.2 s, starting and ending at rest, using a trapezoidal velocity profile with equal acceleration and deceleration periods of 0.3 s each. Cruise time = 1.2 − 0.6 = 0.6 s. If the cruise velocity is v, the distance = ½v(0.3) + v(0.6) + ½v(0.3) = 0.15v + 0.6v + 0.15v = 0.9v. So v = 0.6/0.9 = 0.667 m/s, and acceleration a = v/t = 0.667/0.3 = 2.22 m/s². For a 20 kg moving mass, force F = ma = 44.4 N plus friction.

2.Rotational Motion

Definition

Rotational motion is the motion of a rigid body in which all its particles describe circles about a common fixed line called the axis of rotation. Different particles have different linear velocities, but all have the same angular velocity.

Rotation is the dominant form of motion in robots, because almost all robot joints are revolute and almost all motors produce rotation. Even a prismatic joint is usually driven by a rotary motor through a lead screw.

Angular quantities

  • Angular displacement θ — measured in radians. One revolution = 2π rad = 360°.
  • Angular velocity ω = dθ/dt, in rad/s. If N is the speed in rpm, ω = 2πN/60.
  • Angular acceleration α = dω/dt, in rad/s².
  • Torque T = F × r × sinφ, in N m, where r is the distance from the axis and φ the angle between the force and the radius. Torque is the rotational equivalent of force.
  • Angular momentum = Iω.
  • Rotational kinetic energy = ½Iω².
  • Power P = Tω = 2πNT/60, where N is in rpm and T in N m.
Formula

Equations of uniformly accelerated rotational motionω = ω₀ + α tθ = ω₀ t + ½ α t²ω² = ω₀² + 2 α θNewton's second law for rotation: T = I αThese are exactly parallel to the linear equations with s → θ, v → ω, a → α, m → I and F → T.

Connecting linear and angular quantities

For a point at radius r from the axis of rotation:

s = r θ , v = r ω , at = r α , ac = v²/r = r ω²

where at is the tangential (linear) acceleration and ac is the centripetal (radial) acceleration directed towards the axis. The centripetal force needed is Fc = m v²/r = m r ω². This matters in robot design because a spinning link loads its bearings radially, and the load rises with the square of the speed.

Example

A robot link rotates at 300 rpm and its centre of mass is 0.3 m from the joint axis. ω = 2π(300)/60 = 31.4 rad/s. Centripetal acceleration = rω² = 0.3 × 31.4² = 296 m/s², which is about 30 times gravity. If the link has a mass of 4 kg, the radial force on the bearing is 4 × 296 = 1184 N.

3.Moment of Inertia

Definition

The moment of inertia I of a body about an axis is the measure of its resistance to angular acceleration. It is the rotational equivalent of mass. For a system of particles, I = Σ m r², and for a continuous body, I = ∫ r² dm, where r is the perpendicular distance of the mass element from the axis of rotation. Its unit is kg m².

The key point is the square of the distance. Mass placed far from the axis contributes far more to the inertia than the same mass placed close to it. A thin ring and a solid disc of the same mass and radius have moments of inertia of m r² and ½ m r² respectively — the ring is twice as hard to spin.

Formula

Moments of inertia of common shapes (about the stated axis)Point mass at distance r: I = m r²Thin ring / thin-walled cylinder: I = m r²Solid disc / solid cylinder: I = ½ m r²Solid sphere: I = (2/5) m r²Hollow sphere: I = (2/3) m r²Thin rod, axis through centre: I = m L²/12Thin rod, axis through one end: I = m L²/3Rectangular plate about a central axis: I = m (a² + b²)/12

Parallel axis theorem

The moment of inertia about any axis parallel to an axis through the centre of mass is

I = IG + m d²

where IG is the moment of inertia about the centroidal axis and d is the perpendicular distance between the two axes. This theorem is essential in robot design, because a robot link almost always rotates about a joint at one end rather than about its centre of mass. For a uniform rod of length L and mass m rotating about one end, I = mL²/12 + m(L/2)² = mL²/12 + mL²/4 = mL²/3.

Radius of gyration

The radius of gyration k is the distance from the axis at which the whole mass could be concentrated to give the same moment of inertia: I = m k², so k = √(I/m). It is a convenient way to compare the distribution of mass in different links.

Why moment of inertia dominates robot design

From T = Iα, the torque a joint motor must produce is directly proportional to the inertia of everything the joint has to move. For a robot arm this includes the link itself, all the links and motors beyond it, the end effector and the workpiece. Because inertia grows with the square of the length, a robot arm that is twice as long needs about four times the torque for the same angular acceleration. This is why robot designers do everything possible to reduce inertia:

  • Motors are mounted as close to the base as possible, and motion is transmitted to the distant joints by belts, chains or tendons. This is the arrangement used in SCARA and Delta robots, where the Delta's motors are all on the fixed base so that the moving parts are extremely light.
  • Links are made hollow, ribbed or of composite material.
  • Links are tapered, being thicker near the joint and thinner at the far end.
  • Light aluminium alloys and carbon fibre are used instead of steel.
  • The payload is specified carefully and never exceeded.
  • Cables and hoses are routed along the arm and supported so that they do not swing.

Inertia reflected through a gearbox

When a load with inertia IL is connected to a motor through a gearbox of ratio n (motor speed divided by load speed), the inertia seen by the motor is

Ireflected = IL / n² and Tmotor = TL / n (neglecting losses)

This is one of the most useful results in robot design. A gearbox of ratio 100:1 reduces the reflected inertia by a factor of 10 000, so the motor only has to accelerate its own rotor inertia plus one ten-thousandth of the load inertia. This is exactly why robot joints use harmonic drives and planetary gearboxes with high ratios: they allow a small, fast, low-inertia motor to control a large, heavy link precisely.

Note

Inertia matching. Servo motor manufacturers specify a recommended ratio of load inertia to motor rotor inertia, typically 5:1 to 10:1 for high-dynamic applications and up to 30:1 for less demanding ones. If the ratio is too high, the servo loop becomes difficult to tune, the response is sluggish and the arm may oscillate. The gearbox is chosen to bring the ratio into the recommended range.

Example

Numerical. A robot joint must accelerate a link of moment of inertia 0.8 kg m² at 20 rad/s². Torque required at the joint = Iα = 0.8 × 20 = 16 N m. If a harmonic drive of ratio 100:1 and efficiency 85 % is used, the motor torque = 16/(100 × 0.85) = 0.188 N m, and the inertia reflected to the motor = 0.8/100² = 8 × 10−5 kg m². A small servo motor with a rotor inertia of 1 × 10−4 kg m² therefore has an inertia ratio of about 1.8:1, which is excellent.

Dynamic torque requirement of a joint

The total torque a robot joint motor must supply is the sum of several terms:

Ttotal = Tinertia + Tfriction + Tgravity + Tpayload + Texternal
  • Tinertia = Iα — to accelerate the link, the motor rotors beyond it, the end effector and the workpiece.
  • Tfriction — to overcome bearing, seal and gear friction; usually 5 % to 15 % of the total.
  • Tgravity = m g r cosθ — to hold the weight of the link against gravity; this is maximum when the arm is horizontal and zero when it is vertical. Shoulder joints suffer most.
  • Tpayload — the effect of the weight and inertia of the carried object.
  • Texternal — process forces such as grinding, pressing or welding contact force.
  • In addition, centrifugal and Coriolis terms appear when several joints move together, because the motion of one joint changes the inertia seen by another.
Important

Gravity torque is often the largest term for the lower joints of a robot arm, especially at full reach with a full payload. It must be supplied continuously even when the robot is holding still, which wastes energy and heats the motor. Many robots therefore include gravity compensation in the controller (the motor current is offset to cancel gravity), or use a mechanical counterbalance, a gas spring or a brake that holds the arm when the motor is idle.

4.Other Elementary Concepts Used in Robot Mechanics

  • Centre of mass — the point at which the whole mass of a body can be considered to act. For a robot link it should be as close to the joint axis as possible to reduce gravity and centrifugal loading.
  • Centre of gravity — the point through which the resultant weight acts. It coincides with the centre of mass in a uniform gravitational field.
  • Static and dynamic balance — a rotating link must be balanced, otherwise it produces a rotating force that vibrates the structure and destroys the bearings.
  • Stress, strain and Young's modulus — σ = F/A, ε = ΔL/L and E = σ/ε. Links must be stiff enough not to bend under load.
  • Deflection — the elastic deformation of a link under load. Deflection at the tool point directly produces positioning error and cannot be corrected by the joint encoders, because they are on the motor side of the gearbox.
  • Torsional stiffness — the resistance of a shaft or a gearbox to twisting. It determines how much the tool position lags behind the commanded position when a force is applied.
  • Natural frequency — f = (1/2π)√(k/I). If the motion of the robot excites this frequency, resonance occurs. Robot structures are designed to have natural frequencies well above the motion frequencies.
  • Friction — static friction (stiction) must be overcome before motion starts; Coulomb (dry) friction is constant and opposes motion; viscous friction is proportional to velocity. Friction causes following error, limit cycling and stick-slip motion.
  • Lubrication — reduces friction and wear; robot gearboxes are filled with special grease for life.

★Summary

  • Translation is motion in which every particle moves the same distance in the same direction; rotation is motion in which every particle describes a circle about a common axis.
  • Linear and rotational quantities are connected by s = rθ, v = rω, at = rα and ac = rω².
  • The rotational equations mirror the linear ones with θ, ω, α, I and T replacing s, v, a, m and F, and Newton's second law becomes T = Iα.
  • Moment of inertia I = Σ m r² measures resistance to angular acceleration; the square of the distance makes mass distribution critical.
  • A ring has twice the moment of inertia of a solid disc of the same mass and radius.
  • The parallel axis theorem I = IG + md² is used because robot links rotate about a joint, not about their centre of mass.
  • A gearbox of ratio n reduces the reflected inertia to IL/n², which is why high-ratio reducers let small motors control heavy links.
  • Joint torque = inertia torque + friction + gravity + payload + external process forces; gravity torque is often the largest for shoulder joints.
  • Designers reduce inertia by mounting motors near the base, by using belts and tendons, by hollow tapered links and by light alloys.
  • Friction, deflection, torsional stiffness and natural frequency all affect the accuracy and smoothness of a robot.
