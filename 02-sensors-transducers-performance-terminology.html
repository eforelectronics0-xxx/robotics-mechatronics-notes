02Sensors and Transducers — Performance Terminology

The difference between a sensor and a transducer, how signals travel through a measurement chain, and the complete set of static and dynamic terms used to judge the performance of any sensor.

1.Sensor, Transducer and the Measurement Chain

Definition

A sensor is a device that detects a physical quantity (the measurand) and responds to it in some way. A transducer is a device that converts energy from one form into another. In measurement work the two words are used almost interchangeably, because a sensor used for measurement always converts the measured quantity into an electrical signal.

Strictly speaking, every sensor is a transducer, but not every transducer is a sensor. A loudspeaker converts electrical energy into sound, so it is a transducer but not a sensor. A microphone converts sound into an electrical signal, so it is both a sensor and a transducer. Similarly, a thermocouple converts a temperature difference directly into a voltage, so it is a self-generating or active transducer. A thermistor does not generate any voltage; it only changes its resistance when heated, and needs an external power supply, so it is a passive or modulating transducer.

The quantity being measured is called the measurand. The input to the transducer is the measurand and the output is an electrical signal. The ratio of output to input is the sensitivity. A sensor that produces a large output for a small input is more sensitive and is easier to interface.

2.Static Characteristics

Static characteristics describe the performance of a sensor when the measured quantity is constant or changing very slowly with time. These are the terms that appear on a datasheet and that a buyer compares when choosing a sensor.

Accuracy

Accuracy tells us how close the indicated value is to the true value of the measurand. It is usually expressed as a percentage of full scale reading or as a percentage of the measured value. For example, a thermometer with a range of 0–100 °C and an accuracy of ±1 % of full scale may be in error by up to 1 °C anywhere in the range. Accuracy is not the same as precision.

Precision and Repeatability

Precision is the closeness with which repeated measurements of the same quantity agree with each other. A sensor can be very precise but not accurate — it may give the same wrong reading every time. Repeatability is the ability of a sensor to give the same output when the same input is applied repeatedly, under the same conditions, in the same direction, over a short period. Reproducibility is the same idea but over a longer period, or after the instrument has been moved or switched off and on.

Error

Error is the difference between the measured value and the true value. Errors are grouped as follows.

  • Systematic error — repeatable and predictable, for example zero offset, wrong calibration or a loading effect. It can be reduced by calibration.
  • Random error — unpredictable, caused by noise, vibration or fluctuations in the supply. It can only be reduced by averaging or better shielding.
  • Gross error — caused by a mistake, such as misreading the scale or a wiring fault.
  • Zero error / offset — the output is not zero when the input is zero.
  • Span error / gain error — the slope of the calibration curve is wrong.

Range and Span

The range is the set of values of the measurand over which the sensor is designed to work, stated as a minimum and maximum, for example −55 °C to +150 °C for an LM35. The span is the algebraic difference between the maximum and minimum, which for the same example is 205 °C. The full scale output (FSO) is the output produced at the maximum input.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity is the ratio of the change in output to the change in input that produced it. It is the slope of the calibration curve.

Sensitivity S = Δ(output) / Δ(input)

For an LM35 temperature sensor the output changes by 10 mV for each 1 °C change, so the sensitivity is 10 mV/°C. A high sensitivity is desirable because small changes become easy to detect, but very high sensitivity also makes the sensor respond to noise and to unwanted quantities.

Linearity and Non-linearity Error

An ideal sensor has a straight-line relationship between input and output. A linear sensor is easy to calibrate because two points are enough to define the whole scale. Real sensors depart from the straight line; this departure is called non-linearity error and is quoted as a percentage of full range. For example a load cell may have a non-linearity of ±0.02 % FSO, which is excellent, while a cheap thermistor may be non-linear by several percent and must be linearised by a circuit or by software.

Hysteresis

Hysteresis is the phenomenon in which the output of a sensor depends not only on the present input but also on the direction from which the input arrived. When the input is increased from zero to full scale and then decreased back, the two curves do not coincide; they form a loop. The maximum difference between the two curves, expressed as a percentage of full range, is the hysteresis error. Hysteresis arises from friction, from magnetic effects (as in eddy current and Hall devices), from elasticity of materials (as in a load cell) and from backlash in gears.

Dead Band and Dead Zone

The dead band (also called dead zone or threshold) is the largest change of input for which the sensor gives no change of output. It is caused by stiction, friction or a minimum energy requirement. For example a pressure switch may not operate until the pressure has risen a little above its nominal set point.

Resolution and Threshold

Resolution is the smallest change in the input that the sensor can detect and indicate. It is often confused with precision but it is different: resolution is about the smallest step that can be seen. In a digital system the resolution is limited by the number of bits of the ADC. For an n-bit converter with a reference voltage Vref,

Resolution = Vref / 2n (for n = 10 and Vref = 5 V, resolution = 5/1024 = 4.88 mV)

Drift and Stability

Drift is an unwanted gradual change in the output that is not caused by a change in the input. It is usually due to ageing of components or to a change in temperature. Zero drift is a shift of the whole calibration curve up or down; sensitivity drift is a change of the slope. Stability is the ability of a sensor to give the same output over a long period for a constant input.

Backlash and Loading Effect

Backlash is the lost motion in a mechanical system, for example the clearance between gear teeth, which causes a small change of input to produce no change of output when the direction of motion is reversed. A loading effect occurs when the act of connecting the measuring instrument changes the quantity being measured. A voltmeter with low input resistance draws current from the circuit and reads a lower voltage than the true one. This is why signal conditioning stages use op-amps with very high input impedance.

3.Dynamic Characteristics

Dynamic characteristics describe how a sensor behaves when the measurand is changing rapidly with time. No sensor can respond instantaneously; there is always some delay because energy must be transferred and stored. Dynamic behaviour is studied by applying a standard test input and observing the output.

The three test inputs

  • Step input — the measurand changes suddenly from one value to another, for example dropping a thermocouple into hot water.
  • Ramp (or linear) input — the measurand changes at a constant rate, for example a temperature rising steadily at 2 °C per minute.
  • Sinusoidal input — the measurand varies sinusoidally at different frequencies, used to find the frequency response.

Response time, Rise time and Time constant

The time constant (τ) of a first-order sensor is the time taken for the output to reach 63.2 % of its final steady value after a step input. After 2τ it reaches 86.5 %, after 3τ it reaches 95 % and after 5τ it reaches 99.3 %, which is usually taken as steady. The response time is the total time taken for the output to reach and stay within a specified band (often ±2 % or ±5 %) of the final value. The rise time is the time taken to go from 10 % to 90 % of the final value.

Note

A small time constant means a fast sensor. A bare thermocouple bead may have τ of a few milliseconds, while the same thermocouple inside a protective sheath may have τ of several seconds.

Time Delay (Dead Time)

Some sensors do not begin to respond at all for a certain period after the input changes. This is called time delay, dead time or transportation lag. A gas sensor placed at the end of a long sampling tube shows a dead time equal to the time the gas takes to travel through the tube.

Overshoot, Settling time and Damping

In a second-order system such as a pressure gauge with a spring and mass, the output may go beyond the final value before coming back. The amount by which it exceeds the final value is the overshoot, usually given as a percentage. The settling time is the time after which the output stays within a specified tolerance band. The damping ratio (ζ) describes how quickly oscillations die out: ζ = 0 gives continuous oscillation, ζ < 1 gives an under-damped response with overshoot, ζ = 1 is critically damped (fastest response without overshoot) and ζ > 1 is over-damped (slow, no overshoot). A damping ratio of about 0.7 is generally considered the best compromise.

Frequency Response and Bandwidth

When a sinusoidal input of increasing frequency is applied, the output amplitude falls and the output phase lags behind the input. The bandwidth is the range of frequencies over which the output amplitude stays within a specified limit, usually within 3 dB (that is, 70.7 %) of the low-frequency value. A sensor must have a bandwidth comfortably higher than the highest frequency present in the measurand, otherwise the reading will be too small and will arrive too late.

Fidelity

Fidelity is the degree to which a sensor reproduces the input waveform without any distortion. A sensor with good fidelity gives an output whose shape matches the input shape; only the amplitude and possibly a time shift differ.

4.Selection of a Sensor

Choosing the right sensor for an application means balancing many factors. The engineer should ask the following questions in order.

  1. What is the measurand and what is its expected range?
  2. What accuracy and resolution does the application really need? Do not over-specify, because accuracy costs money.
  3. Is contact acceptable? A rotating or very hot object may need a non-contact sensor such as an eddy current probe, a Hall sensor or a pyrometer.
  4. How fast does it change? This decides the required time constant and bandwidth.
  5. What is the operating environment? Temperature, humidity, dust, vibration, corrosive chemicals and electromagnetic interference all affect the choice and the enclosure rating.
  6. What output form is needed — analogue voltage, current loop (4–20 mA), digital, PWM or a simple switch contact?
  7. What are the constraints of size, weight, power supply, cost, life and availability?
  8. Does it need calibration and how often?
Example

Suppose the water temperature in a domestic geyser has to be displayed with an accuracy of about 1 °C over 0–100 °C, the water is still, and the cost must be low. A thermocouple would be far too insensitive and would need cold junction compensation. An RTD is very accurate but costly and slow. A thermistor is cheap and sensitive but strongly non-linear. The LM35 is the best choice here: it gives a linear 10 mV/°C output, needs no calibration, works from a single 5 V supply and costs very little. This is exactly the sensor studied in Topic 5.

★Summary

  • A sensor detects a measurand; a transducer converts one form of energy into another. Transducers are active (self-generating) or passive (needing an external supply).
  • Static characteristics apply when the input is steady: accuracy, precision, repeatability, error, range, span, sensitivity, linearity, hysteresis, dead band, resolution, drift, stability, backlash and loading effect.
  • Sensitivity is the slope of the calibration curve, S = Δoutput / Δinput.
  • Hysteresis means the output depends on the direction of approach of the input; dead band is the input change that produces no output change.
  • Dynamic characteristics apply when the input changes with time: time constant, response time, rise time, dead time, overshoot, settling time, damping ratio, frequency response, bandwidth and fidelity.
  • For a first-order sensor the output reaches 63.2 % of its final value in one time constant and 99.3 % in five time constants.
  • A damping ratio of about 0.7 gives the best compromise between speed and overshoot.
  • Sensor selection is a trade-off between accuracy, speed, environment, output form, size and cost.
