04Strain Gauge Load Cell, Tactile Sensor and Float Sensor

How a strain gauge converts force into a change of resistance, how four gauges are arranged in a Wheatstone bridge inside a load cell, how tactile arrays give a robot the sense of touch, and how float sensors measure liquid level.

  2. Wheatstone Bridge and the Load Cell

1.Strain Gauge

Principle

The strain gauge works on the principle that the electrical resistance of a conductor changes when it is mechanically deformed. This is known as the piezoresistive effect. When a wire is stretched, it becomes longer and thinner; both changes increase its resistance. Since resistance is given by R = ρL/A, where ρ is resistivity, L is length and A is cross-sectional area, an increase in L and a decrease in A both raise R.

Formula

Gauge factorGf = (ΔR / R) / (ΔL / L) = (ΔR / R) / εwhere ε = ΔL/L is the strain (dimensionless, often written in microstrain, 1 µε = 10−6). A typical metallic foil gauge has Gf ≈ 2. A higher gauge factor means greater sensitivity. Semiconductor gauges have Gf of 50 to 200 but are more temperature sensitive and more brittle.

Construction and types

  • Bonded metallic wire gauge — a very fine wire (about 25 µm diameter) of constantan or nickel-chromium arranged in a grid and cemented between two thin sheets of backing material.
  • Bonded metallic foil gauge — the most common type. A thin metal foil (5 to 10 µm) is photo-etched into a grid pattern. It has a large surface area so it dissipates heat well, and it is thin so it follows the surface strain faithfully.
  • Unbonded gauge — wires are stretched across a movable frame; used in older instruments and some pressure sensors.
  • Semiconductor gauge — made of silicon; very high gauge factor but strongly affected by temperature.
  • Rosette gauge — three gauges at known angles to measure strain in two dimensions.

Bonding

The gauge must be bonded to the surface whose strain is to be measured, using a special adhesive such as a cyanoacrylate or an epoxy. The surface is first cleaned and abraded. The strain is then transferred from the member to the backing and from the backing to the grid. If the bonding is poor, the gauge will slip and the reading will be wrong.

Note

Strain is very small. A typical load cell may produce only 1000 µε (0.1 %) strain at full load. With Gf = 2, ΔR/R = 2 × 10−3, so a 120 Ω gauge changes by only 0.24 Ω. Such a tiny change cannot be measured with an ohmmeter, which is why a Wheatstone bridge is used.

2.Wheatstone Bridge and the Load Cell

A Wheatstone bridge has four resistive arms. If all four resistances are equal, the bridge is balanced and the output voltage between the two mid-points is zero. When one or more arms change, an output voltage appears which is proportional to the change. In a load cell all four arms are strain gauges, which doubles the sensitivity and cancels the effect of temperature.

For a bridge with excitation voltage Vex across one diagonal and four equal gauges of resistance R,

Vout = (Vex / 4) × Gf × ε (for a full bridge with two gauges in tension and two in compression)

The output is still only a few millivolts, typically 1 mV/V to 3 mV/V of excitation. A load cell rated 2 mV/V with 10 V excitation gives 20 mV at full load. This small signal needs an instrumentation amplifier with a gain of 100 to 1000, high input impedance and a high common-mode rejection ratio. Dedicated ICs such as the HX711 (24-bit ADC with a built-in low-noise amplifier) or the AD620 are commonly used with microcontrollers.

Types of load cell

  • Shear beam / bending beam — gauges bonded to a cantilever or a simply supported beam. Most common in platform scales and bench weighing.
  • Compression / column type — a solid column with gauges along its length. Used for very large loads such as truck weighbridges.
  • S-type (tension-compression) — an S-shaped body that can measure both pulling and pushing forces. Used in hopper and tank weighing.
  • Ring / proving ring — a circular ring that deforms under load; very stable, used as a calibration standard.
  • Pancake / canister — low profile, high capacity.
  • Single point — designed for a small platform where the load may be applied off-centre.

Sources of error in a load cell include temperature changes (compensated by using four gauges and by dummy gauges), off-centre loading, side loads, vibration, creep of the adhesive, and the lead wire resistance in long cables, which is avoided by using a six-wire (sense) connection that measures the voltage actually reaching the bridge.

Applications

Load cells are used in electronic weighing machines from kitchen scales to weighbridges, in force measurement during material testing (universal testing machines), in torque measurement through strain gauges on a shaft, in aircraft landing gear load monitoring, in industrial hoppers and silos, in medical scales and in the joints of some robots to sense the gripping force.

Example

Numerical. A load cell uses four 350 Ω gauges with a gauge factor of 2.1, is excited with 10 V and is subjected to a strain of 800 µε. Output V = (10/4) × 2.1 × 800 × 10−6 = 2.5 × 2.1 × 8 × 10−4 = 4.2 mV. If this is fed to an amplifier of gain 500, the final output is 2.1 V, easily read by a 10-bit ADC.

3.Tactile Sensor

Definition

A tactile sensor is a device that measures the information resulting from contact between the sensor and an object, and it produces a signal that represents the distribution of contact force over the sensing area. While a load cell measures one total force, a tactile sensor measures a two-dimensional pattern of forces, and therefore can tell not only how hard something is being held but also what shape and texture it has.

The words tactile and touch are used differently in robotics. A touch sensor simply indicates whether contact has occurred (binary yes/no), whereas a tactile sensor gives the magnitude and distribution of the contact force. A force/torque sensor measures the three forces and three torques at a single point, usually at a robot wrist.

Types of tactile sensing element

  • Piezoresistive (FSR — force sensitive resistor) — a polymer thick film whose resistance decreases as pressure increases. Very common, cheap, thin, large range (a few grams to several kilograms), but suffers from hysteresis, drift and temperature sensitivity.
  • Conductive elastomer / rubber — a silicone rubber filled with carbon particles. Compression brings the particles closer, lowering the resistance. Used in arrays and in soft gripper pads.
  • Capacitive — two conducting plates separated by a dielectric foam or elastomer. Pressure reduces the gap, so the capacitance increases. Very sensitive, good dynamic response and little hysteresis, but needs complex electronics and is affected by stray fields.
  • Piezoelectric — materials such as PVDF film or quartz generate a charge when deformed. Excellent for measuring changing forces, vibration, slip and texture, but cannot measure a static force because the charge leaks away.
  • Strain gauge array — miniature gauges on a flexible backing, arranged in a matrix.
  • Optical / fibre optic — pressure changes the intensity, phase or wavelength of light in a fibre, or deforms a reflective membrane viewed by a camera.
  • Barometric / pneumatic — an array of small air chambers whose pressure rises on contact; robust in harsh environments.
  • MEMS tactile sensors — micro-machined cantilevers with piezoresistive read-out, used in electronic skin.

Tactile array and signal processing

Practical tactile sensors are built as an array or matrix, typically 8 × 8 to 32 × 32 sensing elements (called taxels, from tactile pixel) with a spacing of 1 mm to 2 mm. Reading every taxel individually would need too many wires, so the array is wired as a matrix and scanned row by row, or a multiplexer such as the CD4051 is used. The microcontroller stores the values as a two-dimensional array which forms a tactile image. From this image the system can compute the centroid of the pressure (to detect slip), the total force, the orientation of an edge, and even recognise the shape of the object being held.

Applications of tactile sensors

  • Robot grippers — to control gripping force so that a fragile object such as an egg or a glass tube is not crushed, and to detect the beginning of slip so the grip can be tightened.
  • Object recognition — identifying shape, size, texture and hardness.
  • Assembly — guiding a peg into a hole, inserting connectors, and detecting proper seating of a part.
  • Safety — sensitive skins on collaborative robots that stop the machine when a person is touched.
  • Prosthetics and teleoperation — giving the operator a sense of feel.
  • Medicine — measuring the pulse, palpation, and pressure distribution under a patient lying in bed to prevent bed sores.
  • Consumer electronics — pressure-sensitive keys and trackpads.
  • Industrial inspection — checking the uniformity of pressure in a press or the seal of a package.

4.Float Sensor (Level Sensor)

A float sensor is the simplest and most widely used device for detecting the level of a liquid in a tank. It works on Archimedes' principle: a body immersed in a liquid is buoyed up by a force equal to the weight of the liquid displaced. A float is made of a material less dense than the liquid (or is a hollow shell), so it always rides on the surface. As the level rises or falls, the float moves with it, and this mechanical movement is converted into an electrical signal.

Types of float sensor

  • Float with lever and mechanical switch — the float is attached to a pivoted arm which opens or closes a microswitch or a mercury switch. Simple and cheap; used in domestic water tanks and in the cistern of a toilet.
  • Vertical float with reed switch — a hollow stem contains one or more reed switches, and the float contains a permanent ring magnet. As the float passes a reed switch, the magnet closes its contacts. Multiple reed switches at different heights give multiple level points. This type is very reliable because the electrical contacts are sealed inside glass and never touch the liquid.
  • Horizontal float switch — mounted in the side wall of the tank; the float swings about a horizontal pivot and operates a reed switch. Used where vertical space is limited.
  • Cable-suspended float — a weighted switch on a cable is dropped into a sump or a sewage pit; when the level rises the float tilts and a mercury or ball switch inside changes state. Used for pump control in drainage pits.
  • Magnetostrictive float — a precision version in which the position of the magnet along a waveguide wire is measured electronically, giving a continuous level reading instead of discrete points. Used in fuel tanks and hydraulic systems.
  • Rotary paddle level switch — for dry solids such as grain or cement: a small paddle rotates freely until the material buries it and stalls the motor, which trips a switch.

Other methods of level measurement

Float sensors are not suitable for every liquid. Where the liquid is sticky, corrosive, or contains solids, other methods are preferred: capacitive probes (the liquid acts as the dielectric), ultrasonic sensors (time of flight of an echo from the surface), radar / microwave, hydrostatic pressure sensors at the bottom of the tank, conductivity probes for water, load cells under the tank, and sight glasses for visual indication.

Interfacing a float switch

A float switch is a simple contact, and it must never be wired directly to a microcontroller pin in an industrial environment. The correct arrangement is: the float switch controls a relay or a contactor coil, and the relay contact gives an isolated signal to the controller. For a small low-voltage reed switch, a pull-up resistor of 10 kΩ to the supply is used, and the switch connects the pin to ground; a small capacitor of 100 nF across the switch reduces contact bounce, and the software also debounces by requiring the signal to be stable for 20 ms to 50 ms before accepting it.

Applications and limitations

  • Applications: automatic control of water level in overhead and underground tanks; pump start/stop control; high-level alarm to prevent overflow; low-level cut-out to protect a pump from running dry; boiler water level; fuel level in vehicles and generators; coffee machines, dishwashers and washing machines; chemical dosing tanks.
  • Limitations: the float can be jammed by debris, scale or ice; it measures only at the point where it is fitted, not the whole tank; density and temperature changes alter the float's immersion depth; moving parts are subject to wear; not suitable for very viscous liquids or slurries.
Example

A typical domestic overhead-tank controller uses two float switches: a low level switch that starts the pump and a high level switch that stops it. The two switches, together with a relay, form a latching circuit so that the pump does not chatter as the water surface waves near a single switch. This arrangement is studied in detail in Topic 17.

★Summary

  • A strain gauge works on the piezoresistive effect: stretching a conductor makes it longer and thinner, so its resistance increases.
  • Gauge factor Gf = (ΔR/R)/ε, about 2 for metallic foil gauges and 50 to 200 for semiconductor gauges.
  • The resistance change is extremely small, so four gauges are used in a Wheatstone bridge; Vout = (Vex/4) Gf ε for a full bridge.
  • A load cell is an elastic member with four bonded gauges, two in tension and two in compression, giving double sensitivity and automatic temperature compensation.
  • Load cell output is only 1 to 3 mV/V and needs an instrumentation amplifier or a dedicated ADC such as the HX711.
  • A tactile sensor measures the distribution of contact force over an area (a tactile image made of taxels), whereas a touch sensor only says whether contact has occurred.
  • Tactile elements include piezoresistive FSRs, conductive elastomers, capacitive, piezoelectric, optical and MEMS types; arrays are scanned as a matrix.
  • A float sensor uses buoyancy to follow a liquid surface and converts the movement into a switch closure, usually through a magnet and a sealed reed switch.
  • Float switches should drive a relay, be debounced in hardware and software, and are unsuitable for viscous or dirty liquids.
