07Mechanical Actuation Systems — Types of Motion and Degrees of Freedom

Why mechanisms are needed in a mechatronic system, the three basic types of motion, how joints and constraints limit movement, and how to count the degrees of freedom of a mechanism.

1.Why Mechanical Actuation is Needed

Most actuators — electric motors, solenoids and cylinders — produce a simple form of motion: continuous rotation or a straight-line stroke. The real machine, however, usually needs something different: a slower speed with a larger torque, a rotation converted into linear travel, an oscillation, an intermittent motion, or a precisely shaped movement such as the dwell-and-rise of a cam. Mechanical actuation systems, also called mechanisms, are the links, gears, belts, cams and screws that stand between the actuator and the load and transform the motion into the required form.

Definition

A mechanism is a combination of rigid or resistant bodies, connected by joints, arranged so that the motion of one member causes predictable motion in the other members. A machine is a mechanism that also transmits or modifies force and does useful work.

The elements of a mechanism are described with a few standard words. A link (or element) is a rigid body that has at least two joints. A joint or pair is the connection between two links that permits a particular relative motion. A kinematic chain is an assembly of links and joints in which the motion of each link is definite with respect to the others. When one link of a kinematic chain is fixed to the frame, the chain becomes a mechanism. If no relative motion is possible at all, the chain is a structure.

2.Types of Motion

Motion in machines is classified into three basic types, and every mechanism is really a device for converting one type into another.

  • Linear or translatory motion — every point of the body moves along a straight line and travels the same distance. Examples: the piston of a cylinder, the plunger of a solenoid, the table of a shaping machine, the carriage of a 3D printer.
  • Rotary motion — the body turns about a fixed axis and every point describes a circle about that axis. Examples: a motor shaft, a road wheel, a lathe spindle, a fan blade. Rotary motion is the natural output of almost all electric motors and is the easiest to transmit over a distance.
  • Oscillatory (or reciprocating angular) motion — the body swings to and fro about a fixed axis through a limited angle. Examples: a pendulum, the wiper arm of a car, the rocker of a four-bar linkage, the needle bar of a sewing machine.
  • Reciprocating motion is the special name used when the to-and-fro motion is along a straight line, as in the piston of an engine.
  • Intermittent motion is motion that alternates between movement and rest, as produced by a Geneva mechanism or a ratchet.
Note

The three important conversions performed by mechanisms are: rotary to rotary (gears, belts, chains), rotary to linear (rack and pinion, lead screw, slider-crank, cam) and linear to rotary (the reverse of the above, as in an engine).

Motion is also described by the way it varies with time. Uniform motion means constant velocity. Uniformly accelerated motion means constant acceleration, which is what a stepper motor gives when it starts. In practice, mechanisms are designed to avoid sudden changes of velocity or acceleration because these produce shock, noise, vibration and wear.

3.Freedom of Constraints and Degrees of Freedom

Definition

The degree of freedom (DOF) of a body is the number of independent directions in which it can move. The freedom of constraint describes how much a joint restricts that movement.

A rigid body floating freely in space can move in six independent ways: it can translate along the X, Y and Z axes and it can rotate about the X, Y and Z axes. Therefore a free body in space has 6 degrees of freedom. A body confined to a plane (planar motion) can translate in two directions and rotate about one axis, so it has 3 degrees of freedom.

Every joint removes some of these freedoms. A revolute (turning) joint allows only rotation about one axis, so it removes five freedoms and leaves one. A prismatic (sliding) joint allows only translation along one axis and also leaves one. A cylindrical joint allows both rotation about and sliding along the same axis, so it leaves two. A spherical (ball and socket) joint allows rotation about three axes and leaves three.

Types of constrained motion

  • Completely constrained motion — the motion is limited to a definite direction by the geometry of the pairing alone, no matter what the direction of the applied force is. Example: a square bar in a square hole can only slide; it cannot rotate. Another example: a shaft with a collar between two bearings can only rotate.
  • Incompletely constrained motion — the pair permits motion in more than one direction, and the actual direction depends on the direction of the applied force. Example: a circular shaft in a circular hole can either rotate or slide. Both motions are possible and neither is prevented by geometry.
  • Successfully constrained motion — the pair is incompletely constrained by geometry, but an external agency such as a spring, gravity or a load removes one of the possible motions, leaving only one. Example: a shaft in a footstep bearing can rotate or lift up; the weight of the shaft (or a spring) keeps it down, so only rotation remains. Another example: the piston in an engine cylinder, held in place by the connecting rod.

Counting the degrees of freedom — Gruebler's criterion

For a planar chain made of rigid links connected by lower pairs, the mobility M (number of independent inputs required) is given by Gruebler's equation, also called the Kutzbach criterion.

M = 3 (n − 1) − 2 j1 − j2

where n is the total number of links including the fixed link, j1 is the number of lower pairs (joints with one degree of freedom such as turning and sliding pairs), and j2 is the number of higher pairs (joints with two degrees of freedom such as a cam and follower or two gear teeth in contact).

  • If M = 0, the chain is a structure — no relative motion is possible.
  • If M = 1, the chain is a mechanism needing one input; most machines are of this type.
  • If M = 2, two independent inputs are required, as in a differential gear.
  • If M is negative, the chain is a statically indeterminate structure with redundant constraints.
Example

Example 1 — four-bar chain. n = 4 links, j1 = 4 turning pairs, j2 = 0. M = 3(4 − 1) − 2(4) − 0 = 9 − 8 = 1. So one input (say the crank) is enough to determine the motion of all other links — it is a mechanism.
Example 2 — slider-crank. n = 4 (crank, connecting rod, slider, frame), j1 = 3 turning pairs + 1 sliding pair = 4. M = 3(3) − 8 = 1.
Example 3 — triangular frame. n = 3, j1 = 3. M = 3(2) − 6 = 0, so it is a structure.
Example 4 — cam and follower. n = 3 (cam, follower, frame), j1 = 2 turning/sliding pairs, j2 = 1 higher pair at the cam contact. M = 3(2) − 2(2) − 1 = 1.

Note

Inversions. If different links of the same kinematic chain are fixed one by one, different mechanisms are obtained. These are called inversions of the chain. The slider-crank chain gives the reciprocating engine (crank fixed as driver), the Whitworth quick-return mechanism and the oscillating cylinder engine. The four-bar chain gives the crank-rocker, the double-crank (drag link) and the double-rocker mechanisms depending on which link is fixed and on the relative lengths (Grashof's law).

★Summary

  • Mechanical actuation systems convert the simple motion of an actuator into the motion actually required by the load.
  • A link is a rigid body with two or more joints; a kinematic chain is an assembly of links with definite relative motion; fixing one link makes it a mechanism.
  • The three basic types of motion are linear (translatory), rotary and oscillatory, plus reciprocating and intermittent motion.
  • Linear and angular quantities are related by s = rθ, v = rω, a = rα, T = F r and P = Tω.
  • A free rigid body in space has 6 degrees of freedom; a body in a plane has 3.
  • Completely constrained motion is limited by geometry alone; incompletely constrained motion depends on the direction of the force; successfully constrained motion is limited by an external agency such as gravity or a spring.
  • Gruebler's equation M = 3(n − 1) − 2j1 − j2 gives the mobility of a planar chain; M = 0 is a structure and M = 1 is a mechanism.
  • Fixing different links of the same chain gives different inversions of that chain.
