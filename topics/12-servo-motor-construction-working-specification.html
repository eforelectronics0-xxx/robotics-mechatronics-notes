12Servo Motor — Construction, Working Principle and Specifications

What makes a servo motor different from an ordinary motor: it is a motor plus a position sensor plus a controller in one closed loop package, able to go to an exact angle and hold it there.

1.What is a Servo Motor?

Definition

A servo motor is a rotary actuator that allows precise control of angular position, velocity and torque. It is not a single device but a system consisting of a motor, a position feedback device, a reduction gearbox and a control circuit, all combined so that the shaft always moves to the commanded position and holds it there against any disturbing force. The word servo comes from the Latin servus, meaning servant — the motor faithfully obeys the command signal.

An ordinary DC motor keeps rotating as long as power is applied; you cannot tell from the motor itself how far it has turned. A servo motor rotates to a specified angle, typically between 0° and 180° for a hobby servo, and stops. If an external force tries to move the shaft away, the servo senses the change and drives the motor to bring it back. This ability to hold a position is what makes servos indispensable in robotics, RC models, CNC machines and industrial automation.

2.Construction

A servo motor unit contains four main parts inside a small rectangular case.

1. The motor

The motor itself is usually a small PMDC motor in hobby servos and low-power industrial servos. It runs at a high speed (several thousand rpm) with low torque. In larger industrial servo motors, a brushless DC (BLDC) or an AC synchronous motor with permanent magnets on the rotor is used, giving higher efficiency, better heat dissipation and longer life. Some special servos use a stepper motor as the base machine, and there are also linear servos in which the rotor is unrolled into a straight moving part.

2. The reduction gearbox

A set of spur gears (plastic in cheap servos, metal in high-torque ones) reduces the motor speed by a ratio of about 30:1 to 300:1 and multiplies the torque by the same factor. This gives the output shaft a useful speed of about 50 to 200 rpm with a torque of a few kg-cm. Planetary or harmonic gearboxes are used in high-precision industrial servos because they have almost zero backlash.

3. The position feedback device

This is what makes the motor a servo. In a hobby servo it is a small potentiometer whose shaft is coupled to the output shaft. As the output rotates, the wiper moves and produces a voltage proportional to the angle. In industrial servos, much more accurate devices are used:

  • Incremental optical encoder — a slotted disc and a photointerrupter produce pulses; position is found by counting pulses from a reference index. Typical resolution 1000 to 10 000 pulses per revolution, multiplied four times by edge decoding.
  • Absolute optical encoder — a coded disc gives a unique digital word for every shaft position, so the position is known immediately at switch-on without any homing. Single-turn and multi-turn versions exist.
  • Resolver — a rugged rotary transformer that gives absolute position and works at high temperature and under vibration; used in aerospace and military applications.
  • Hall sensor array — used in BLDC servos for commutation and coarse position.
  • Potentiometer — cheap and absolute but with limited resolution, wear and noise.

4. The control electronics

A small printed circuit board carries a comparator or, in modern servos, a microcontroller running a PID algorithm. It compares the commanded position with the actual position from the feedback device, computes the error, and drives the motor through an H-bridge in the direction that reduces the error. It also provides PWM speed control, current limiting, soft starting, end-stop protection and sometimes a serial communication interface.

5. The output shaft and case

The output shaft projects from the case with a splined or serrated end to which a servo horn (arm) is screwed. Hobby servos have three wires: red for +5 V supply, black or brown for ground, and orange, yellow or white for the control signal. Industrial servos use connectors for power, encoder feedback and communication.

3.Working Principle

The servo works as a closed loop position control system. The sequence of events is as follows.

  1. A command signal, usually a PWM pulse of width between 1 ms and 2 ms repeated every 20 ms, arrives at the control input. The width represents the desired angle: 1 ms for 0°, 1.5 ms for 90° and 2 ms for 180°.
  2. The control circuit converts this pulse width into a reference voltage Vref proportional to the desired angle.
  3. The potentiometer (or encoder) produces a feedback voltage Vfb proportional to the actual shaft angle.
  4. The error voltage e = Vref − Vfb is computed and amplified by the controller.
  5. If the error is positive, the H-bridge drives the motor in one direction; if negative, in the other direction. The speed of the motor is proportional to the magnitude of the error, so the servo slows down as it approaches the target — this is natural proportional control.
  6. As the shaft turns, the feedback voltage changes, and the error reduces. When the error becomes zero (or falls within the dead band), the motor stops.
  7. If an external force moves the shaft, the feedback voltage changes, an error appears, and the motor drives the shaft back to the commanded position. This is the holding action.

Industrial servos use full PID control with high-resolution encoders to achieve accuracies of a few arc-seconds.

Types of servo motor

  • Positional rotation servo — the standard hobby servo, limited to about 180° by mechanical end stops. Most common.
  • Continuous rotation servo — the end stops are removed and the potentiometer is fixed at mid-position; the pulse width then controls speed and direction instead of position. 1.5 ms is stop, 1 ms is full speed one way, 2 ms is full speed the other way.
  • Linear servo — the output is a linear push-pull motion, obtained either by unrolling the motor or by a screw mechanism.
  • Industrial AC servo — a permanent magnet synchronous motor with an absolute encoder and a dedicated servo amplifier, controlled by position, velocity or torque loop. Used in CNC machines, robots and pick-and-place machines.
  • Digital servo — uses a microcontroller running at several hundred Hz instead of an analogue comparator, giving faster response, tighter holding and smoother motion, but drawing more current.
  • Analog servo — the traditional type, with a response rate of 50 Hz.
Note

Servo versus stepper. A stepper motor moves in fixed steps and, in open loop, assumes that it has arrived; it can lose steps under overload and has poor torque at high speed. A servo motor uses feedback, so it always knows where it is, can correct an error, gives full torque at high speed and runs smoothly and quietly. Servos are used where high speed, high accuracy or high dynamic response is needed; steppers are used where cost matters and the speed is low.

Standard servo sizes

  • Micro servo (9 g) — about 23 × 12 × 25 mm, torque 1.5 to 2.5 kg-cm. Used in small RC models, pan-tilt camera mounts and lightweight robot arms.
  • Standard servo (40 g to 60 g) — about 40 × 20 × 38 mm, torque 3 to 10 kg-cm. The most common size in hobby robotics.
  • Large / winch servo — torque 15 to 60 kg-cm, metal gears, used in steering of large models and in robot joints.
  • Industrial servo — rated in watts and newton metres, with a matching amplifier and encoder, used in CNC machines and robots.

Selecting a servo

The engineer must first calculate the load torque (friction plus gravity plus acceleration torque) and the required speed, then add a safety margin of 50 % to 100 % on torque. The load inertia reflected to the motor shaft should be matched to the motor inertia, usually keeping the ratio below 10:1 for good response. Other factors are the required accuracy and repeatability, the duty cycle, the supply available, the mounting space, the environmental conditions and the cost.

Example

Torque calculation example. A robot arm link of weight 5 N has its centre of gravity 0.15 m from the servo shaft. The static torque required to hold the arm horizontal is T = W × r = 5 × 0.15 = 0.75 N m = 7.65 kg-cm. Allowing a safety factor of 1.5 for acceleration and friction, a servo of at least 11.5 kg-cm is required.

Applications of servo motors

  • Robotics — joint actuators of educational, hobby and light industrial robots; gripper drives; mobile robot steering.
  • CNC machines and machine tools — axis drives for milling, turning, grinding and EDM machines, where position accuracy of a few micrometres is required.
  • Radio-controlled models — control surfaces of aircraft, steering and throttle of cars, and sail winches of boats. This was the original application.
  • Industrial automation — pick-and-place machines, packaging machinery, assembly robots, conveyors with precise indexing, welding robots and painting robots.
  • Aerospace and defence — flight control surface actuators (fly-by-wire), antenna and radar positioning, missile fin control, turret drives.
  • Automotive — electronic power steering, throttle control, active suspension, headlight levelling, seat and mirror adjustment.
  • Medical — surgical robots, infusion pumps, imaging equipment positioning, prosthetic limbs and exoskeletons.
  • Consumer electronics — camera autofocus and zoom, disc drive positioning, DVD tray, hard disc head positioning (voice coil servo), smart locks and valve actuators.
  • Textile, printing and paper industries — precise tension and register control.

★Summary

  • A servo motor is a complete closed loop positioning system: motor, reduction gearbox, position feedback device and control electronics in one package.
  • The feedback device may be a potentiometer (hobby servos), an incremental or absolute optical encoder, or a resolver (industrial servos).
  • Working: the command pulse width is converted to a reference, compared with the feedback, and the error drives the motor through an H-bridge until the error is zero. If the shaft is disturbed, the servo corrects it.
  • A hobby servo uses a 20 ms period PWM signal: 1 ms = 0°, 1.5 ms = 90°, 2 ms = 180°. A continuous-rotation servo uses the same signal for speed and direction.
  • Industrial servos use PID control with high-resolution encoders and are commanded by analogue voltage, pulse trains or an industrial fieldbus.
  • Key specifications are torque (in kg-cm or N m), speed, resolution, accuracy, repeatability, dead band, gear type, current drain and duty.
  • Servos differ from stepper motors in that they use feedback, so they never lose position, they run smoothly at high speed and they can correct an error.
  • Applications include robot joints, CNC axes, RC models, industrial automation, aerospace actuators, automotive steering, medical devices and camera drives.
