29End Effectors and Force Control — Static Force/Torque, Constraints, Stiffness and Compliance

The devices at the end of a robot arm and how a robot controls contact force: grippers and tools, the Jacobian transpose relation, natural and artificial constraints, and the crucial idea of compliance.

1.End Effectors

Definition

An end effector is the device mounted on the robot wrist that actually performs the task. It is the “hand” of the robot. The end effector is not part of the robot itself; it is chosen and designed for the specific job, and it is often changed several times during a shift.

End effectors fall into two families: grippers, which hold and move a workpiece, and tools, which perform an operation on the workpiece such as welding, painting, cutting or drilling. A third category is sensors mounted at the wrist, such as a force/torque sensor or a camera, which are not themselves effectors but are mounted in the same position.

Grippers

  • Parallel jaw gripper — two flat fingers that close in a straight line. The most common type. It grips by friction or by enclosing the part between shaped fingers. Driven by a pneumatic cylinder, an electric motor through a screw or a cam, or a solenoid. Two fingers are standard; three fingers are used for cylindrical parts.
  • Angular (swinging or pivoting) gripper — the fingers pivot about a point, so they can grip a part from outside or from inside (internal gripping of a hole or a tube). Useful where straight-line access is not available.
  • Scissor (shear) gripper — fingers that cross, for thin or delicate items.
  • Vacuum (suction) gripper — a rubber, polyurethane or foam cup is pressed against the part and the air is evacuated by a venturi ejector or an electric pump. Excellent for flat, smooth, non-porous parts such as glass, sheet metal, tiles, plastic panels and cardboard. Very light and fast, and it does not damage the surface. It fails on porous, oily, very hot or very small parts.
  • Magnetic gripper — a permanent magnet with a mechanical release, or an electromagnet. Used for ferrous sheet metal, discs and rings. Very strong and simple, but residual magnetism can make release difficult, and it cannot be used near sensitive electronics.
  • Adhesive / hook-and-loop gripper — for very light items such as fabric.
  • Bernoulli gripper — uses a high-speed air flow to create a lifting force without contact; used for silicon wafers and delicate sheets.
  • Needle / pin gripper — many small pins retract into a compliant surface and lock around the object; used for textiles, foam and irregular soft items.
  • Soft / compliant gripper — elastomer fingers actuated by air, fluid or shape-memory alloy. They conform to the shape of the object and can grip delicate, irregular items such as fruit, eggs and cables.
  • Dexterous (multi-fingered) hand — three to five fingers with several joints each, driven by tendons or small motors, with tactile sensing. Used in research and in advanced assembly.
  • Collet, chuck and expanding mandrel — for holding cylindrical parts and for internal gripping.

Gripper design considerations

  1. Gripping force. Calculate the force needed to hold the part against gravity and against the acceleration of the robot. For a friction grip, Fgrip = (m atotal) / (μ n), where atotal is the resultant of gravity and the maximum acceleration, μ is the coefficient of friction and n the number of fingers. A safety factor of 2 to 3 is applied to cover oil, vibration and wear.
  2. Stroke. The finger travel must accommodate the tolerance on the part size and must allow easy entry.
  3. Speed. Opening and closing time adds to the cycle time. Pneumatic grippers are fastest.
  4. Accuracy and repeatability of the finger position, which affects how the part is placed.
  5. Weight. The gripper counts as payload and adds inertia, so it must be as light as possible.
  6. Sensing. Open/close confirmation switches are essential; force sensing, part-present sensing and slip detection are used for higher-quality applications.
  7. Fail-safe behaviour. On loss of air or power the gripper should not drop the part. Spring-applied and normally-closed designs, or check valves that trap the air, are used.
  8. Environment. Cleanroom, food-grade, high temperature, explosive atmosphere or washdown may all be required.
  9. Changeover. A tool changer allows one robot to switch automatically between several end effectors, with automatic connection of air, power and signals.
Example

Gripping force calculation. A part of mass 2 kg is held by a two-finger gripper with a coefficient of friction of 0.15. The robot may accelerate at up to 2 g = 19.6 m/s². The worst case is when the part is held horizontally and accelerated vertically upward: the total force to be resisted is m(g + a) = 2(9.81 + 19.6) = 58.8 N. Each finger provides friction μF, and there are two fingers, so 2 × 0.15 × F = 58.8, giving F = 196 N. With a safety factor of 2, the gripper must supply 392 N per finger. This shows why friction gripping of a heavy part at high acceleration needs a very powerful gripper, and why a shaped (encapsulating) finger that does not rely on friction is often a better solution.

Tools

  • Welding — a spot welding gun (heavy, water cooled, with a transformer) or an arc welding torch with a wire feeder and gas supply.
  • Painting and coating — a spray gun with atomising air, a paint supply and an electrostatic charging electrode. The whole robot must be explosion proof.
  • Cutting — laser head, plasma torch, waterjet nozzle, ultrasonic knife, routing spindle, shearing blade.
  • Material removal — grinding wheel, deburring tool, polishing and buffing spindle, wire brush.
  • Assembly — nut runner and screwdriver with torque and angle monitoring, riveting gun, press, adhesive dispensing gun, soldering iron, ultrasonic welding horn.
  • Dispensing — glue, sealant, silicone, solder paste and encapsulant guns with metering.
  • Inspection — camera, laser scanner, ultrasonic probe, feeler gauge, touch probe.

2.Why Force Control is Needed

Ordinary position control assumes that the robot moves freely in space. As soon as the tool touches something, that assumption fails. If a position-controlled robot is commanded to move 1 mm into a rigid surface, it will try to do so, and because the structure is stiff the resulting force can be enormous — enough to break the tool, damage the part or damage the robot. This is the fundamental problem of contact tasks.

Many industrial tasks are defined by force rather than by position: polishing must apply a constant pressure; assembly must push a peg in without jamming; grinding must maintain a constant normal force so the removal rate is uniform; deburring must follow an uneven edge at constant force; and a robot handing an object to a person must be gentle. These tasks need force control.

3.Static Force and Torque Relationships

When the robot is stationary and the tool is in contact with the environment, a force and torque F acts at the tool centre point. Each joint must supply a torque to resist it. The relationship is given by the transpose of the Jacobian:

τ = JT(θ) F

where τ is the n × 1 vector of joint torques, J is the 6 × n Jacobian matrix and F is the 6 × 1 vector of forces and torques at the tool, expressed in the base frame:

F = [ Fx Fy Fz Mx My Mz ]T τ = [ τ1 τ2 … τn ]T

This relation is the principle of virtual work: the work done by the joint torques through the joint displacements equals the work done by the tool force through the tool displacement. It works in both directions:

  • From force to torque (τ = JTF) — used to calculate the motor torque needed to apply a given process force, and in joint torque control where the controller commands a torque instead of a position.
  • From torque to force (F = (JT)−1τ) — used to estimate the contact force from the measured motor currents, which is how a robot without a wrist sensor can still detect a collision. This is called sensorless force estimation and is now standard in collaborative robots.

For a single joint the relation reduces to the familiar lever rule, τ = F r sinφ, where r is the distance from the joint axis to the point of application and φ the angle between the force and the radius. This means the same tool force requires more joint torque when the arm is stretched out than when it is folded in — another reason to keep the working points close to the robot base.

Example

Numerical. A grinding robot must apply a normal force of 150 N at the tool. The tool is 0.9 m horizontally from joint 2 and 0.5 m from joint 3. The static torque required at joint 3 is 150 × 0.5 = 75 N m, and at joint 2 it is 150 × 0.9 = 135 N m, in addition to the torque needed to support the weight of the arm and the grinder. This is why grinding robots are rated for a large payload even though the grinder itself is light.

4.Natural and Artificial Constraints

The idea of constraints was formalised by Mason and by Raibert and Craig in the development of hybrid position/force control. A task is described by listing which directions of motion are constrained by the environment and which are free.

Definition

Natural constraints are those imposed by the geometry and physics of the task itself. They exist whether or not a robot is present, and they cannot be violated. They define which motions are possible and which forces can be applied.

Artificial constraints are those imposed by the control system or by the programmer in order to perform the task in the desired way. They are a choice, and they can be changed by rewriting the program.

Examples of natural constraints

  • Peg in a hole. A round peg of diameter slightly less than the hole can slide freely along the hole axis (z) and can rotate about that axis. It cannot move in x or y, and it cannot tilt about x or y without jamming. So the natural constraints are: velocity constrained to zero in x, y, and in rotation about x and y; force constrained to zero in z and in rotation about z. The task is to move along z (free) while applying no force in x and y (constrained).
  • Wiping a surface. Motion perpendicular to the surface is impossible (position constrained); motion along the surface is free; a normal force must be applied but the tangential force is not specified.
  • Turning a crank or a handwheel. The hand is constrained to move on a circle; the radial force is unconstrained.
  • Inserting a key, screwdriving, opening a door, pulling a drawer, polishing, deburring, writing on a whiteboard — each has its own set of natural constraints.
  • Rolling without slipping — a non-holonomic constraint, because it restricts velocities but not positions.

Examples of artificial constraints

  • Motion along the free directions at a specified speed — the programmer chooses how fast to push the peg in.
  • Force along the constrained directions — the programmer chooses to apply 20 N of polishing pressure.
  • Joint limits, soft limits and forbidden zones programmed into the controller.
  • Maximum speed and maximum force limits for safety, especially in collaborative robots.
  • Compliance or stiffness values set in the servo loop for each direction.
  • The choice of the task frame — the coordinate system in which the constraints are expressed. This is an arbitrary choice by the programmer, and choosing it well makes the task description very simple.
  • The path taken between two points.
  • The selection matrix in hybrid control, which decides for each of the six directions whether position or force is controlled.

Hybrid position/force control

The classical solution is to divide the six directions of the task frame into two sets: those in which position is controlled (the naturally constrained directions, where motion is impossible, so we command a force) and those in which force is controlled (the naturally free directions, where motion is possible, so we command a position or a velocity). A diagonal selection matrix S with 0s and 1s does the switching:

u = S [ force controller output ] + (I − S) [ position controller output ]

For peg-in-hole, S selects force control in x, y, roll and pitch, and position control in z and yaw. For surface polishing, S selects force control along the surface normal and position control in the two tangential directions and in the orientations.

Note

In practice, the task frame must be aligned with the constraints. If the hole is tilted 5° from the robot's z axis and the controller uses the robot frame, it will command motion in a direction that is partly constrained, and it will jam. This is why the frame must be defined carefully, sometimes by first probing the surface to find its orientation.

5.Stiffness and Compliance

Definition

Stiffness k is the force needed to produce a unit displacement: k = F/x, measured in N/m (or in N m/rad for rotational stiffness). Compliance c is the reciprocal: c = x/F = 1/k, measured in m/N. A stiff system barely moves under load; a compliant system moves easily.

Why compliance is essential for contact tasks

Consider a rigid robot trying to insert a peg into a hole with a clearance of only 0.1 mm. If the hole is misplaced by 0.2 mm, a rigid position-controlled robot will simply push harder, generating a force of thousands of newtons and jamming or breaking something. If the wrist is compliant in the lateral directions, the same 0.2 mm error produces only a small lateral force, and that force tilts the peg so that it finds the hole. Compliance converts a position error into a harmless small force and a self-correcting motion.

The same principle explains why a person can insert a key by feel: the fingers and wrist are compliant, so the key is guided into the slot by the contact forces themselves rather than by precise positioning.

Passive compliance

Compliance built into the hardware, with no sensing or control.

  • Elastomer pads and spring mounts — a rubber or spring element between the wrist and the tool. Cheap and simple, but the compliance is the same in all directions unless shaped carefully.
  • Remote Centre Compliance (RCC) device — the classic passive compliant wrist. It consists of two plates separated by elastomer shear pads arranged so that the compliance centre is located remotely, near the tip of the peg. A lateral force at the peg tip then produces a small lateral displacement and a small tilt, which guides the peg into the hole instead of jamming it. The axial direction remains stiff, so the peg can be pushed in. RCC devices made automatic assembly possible in the 1970s and 1980s and are still used.
  • Compliant fingers and soft grippers — foam, rubber or elastomer finger pads.
  • Bellows, flexures and notch hinges — precisely designed elastic elements that give compliance in chosen directions only.
  • Series elastic actuator (SEA) — a spring placed deliberately between the motor gearbox and the load. The spring deflection measures the torque, and the compliance makes the robot safe on impact and able to store and release energy. Used in legged robots such as those developed at MIT and in modern humanoids and exoskeletons.
  • Inherent compliance — the elasticity of the links, gears and bearings. It is always present but is usually too small and too unpredictable to rely on.

Active compliance

Compliance produced by the control system rather than by hardware. A force/torque sensor in the wrist measures the actual contact force, and the controller adjusts the commanded position so that the force equals the desired value. This is far more flexible than passive compliance: the stiffness can be programmed separately in each of the six directions, it can be changed during the task, and it can be made non-linear.

  • Impedance control — the robot behaves as if it were a mass-spring-damper system with a programmed stiffness, damping and inertia. It does not track a force exactly but produces a natural, stable interaction. Widely used in assembly, polishing and human-robot interaction.
  • Admittance control — the measured force is used to modify the commanded position; the robot “gives way” when pushed. Used in collaborative robots and in hand-guiding teach mode.
  • Explicit force control — a PID loop on the measured force, used where the force must be held exactly, as in grinding.
  • Hybrid position/force control — as described above, with a selection matrix.
  • Stiffness matrix control — a 6 × 6 matrix specifying the stiffness in and between all directions, giving a full description of the compliant behaviour.

Compliance and stability

There is an important trade-off. A compliant robot is safe and forgiving but is less accurate and responds more slowly. A stiff robot is accurate and fast but is dangerous on contact and will jam in assembly. Also, when a robot with a force sensor contacts a stiff environment, the force control loop can become unstable and chatter, because the sensor, the structure and the environment form a resonant system. Reducing the force loop gain, adding damping, filtering the force signal and increasing the compliance all improve stability. This is one of the classic problems of force control.

Force control applications

  • Assembly — peg-in-hole insertion, snap fitting, connector mating, gear meshing, bearing installation.
  • Surface finishing — grinding, polishing, deburring, sanding and buffing, where a constant normal force is essential for a uniform finish.
  • Machining — robotic milling and drilling with force-limited feed.
  • Testing — pressing, spring testing, insertion force measurement, tactile probing.
  • Medical — robotic surgery, ultrasound scanning and rehabilitation, where the contact force with the patient must be safe and constant.
  • Human-robot collaboration — detecting contact with a person and stopping or yielding; hand-guiding teach mode.
  • Walking and legged robots — controlling the foot contact force and the leg impedance.
  • Winding, tensioning and unwinding — maintaining constant tension in a wire, a tape or a textile.
  • Welding and sealing — maintaining a constant stand-off distance or contact force with the torch or nozzle.

★Summary

  • An end effector is the device at the wrist that performs the task; it belongs to one of two families, grippers or tools.
  • Grippers include parallel jaw, angular, scissor, vacuum, magnetic, Bernoulli, needle, soft and dexterous multi-fingered types.
  • Gripping force for a friction grip is F = ma/(μn), with a safety factor of 2 to 3; a shaped encapsulating finger is better than friction for heavy parts.
  • Gripper design must consider force, stroke, speed, weight, sensing, fail-safe behaviour and the environment; a tool changer allows automatic switching.
  • Tools include welding guns, spray guns, cutting heads, grinding spindles, nut runners and dispensing nozzles.
  • Force control is needed because a rigid position-controlled robot in contact with a rigid environment generates very large forces.
  • The static relationship between tool force and joint torque is τ = JTF; the reverse, F = (JT)−1τ, allows sensorless force estimation from motor currents.
  • Natural constraints are imposed by the geometry of the task and cannot be violated; artificial constraints are imposed by the control system and can be changed by reprogramming.
  • Hybrid position/force control uses a selection matrix to control force in the naturally constrained directions and position in the free directions.
  • Stiffness k = F/x and compliance c = x/F = 1/k. Compliance converts a position error into a small harmless force.
  • Passive compliance is built into hardware: elastomer pads, flexures, the Remote Centre Compliance device and series elastic actuators.
  • Active compliance uses a wrist force/torque sensor with impedance, admittance or explicit force control, and is programmable in all six directions.
  • Force control loops can chatter against a stiff environment, so gain, damping and filtering must be chosen carefully.
