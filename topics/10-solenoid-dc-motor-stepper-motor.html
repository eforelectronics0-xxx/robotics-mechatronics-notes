10Solenoids, Brushed DC Motor and Stepper Motor

Three electrical actuators in detail: the solenoid that produces a short straight-line stroke, the brushed DC motor whose speed and torque are easily controlled, and the stepper motor that moves in exact digital steps without feedback.

On this page

  1. Solenoid
  2. Brushed DC Motor
  3. Stepper Motor

1.Solenoid

Definition

A solenoid is an electromagnetic actuator that converts electrical energy into a short, straight-line mechanical stroke. It consists of a coil of insulated copper wire wound on a former and a movable soft iron core called the plunger or armature.

Working principle

When a current flows through the coil, a magnetic field is set up along its axis, and the coil behaves like a bar magnet. The soft iron plunger inside the field becomes magnetised by induction and experiences a force that pulls it towards the centre of the coil, where the flux density is greatest. A spring usually returns the plunger when the current is switched off. The force on the plunger is approximately

F = (B² A) / (2 μ0) and since B ∝ (N I)/g , F ∝ (N I)² / g²

where B is the flux density in the air gap, A is the area of the pole face, μ0 = 4π × 10−7 H/m, N is the number of turns, I the current and g the length of the air gap. The important conclusion is that the force increases rapidly as the air gap closes, so a solenoid gives its maximum force at the end of its stroke, not at the beginning.

Types of solenoid

  • Linear (pull type) solenoid — the plunger is pulled into the coil; the most common type.
  • Push type solenoid — the plunger is pushed out, using a reversed magnetic arrangement.
  • Rotary solenoid — a disc with inclined grooves and balls converts the linear pull into a rotation of about 25° to 45°.
  • Latching (bi-stable) solenoid — a permanent magnet holds the plunger in position, so current is needed only to change the state. Saves power and heat.
  • Proportional solenoid — the force is proportional to the current over a useful part of the stroke, allowing proportional control of a valve.
  • Tubular, U-frame and E-frame — different mechanical constructions giving different stroke and force combinations.
  • Voice coil actuator — a permanent magnet and a moving coil; gives smooth, fast, precisely controllable motion over a short stroke, as in a hard disc drive or a loudspeaker.

Applications

  • Solenoid valves controlling water, air, oil, gas and steam — in washing machines, dishwashers, irrigation, pneumatic systems and process plant.
  • Relays, contactors and circuit breakers (the operating mechanism is a solenoid).
  • Door locks in automobiles and in electronic access control systems.
  • Starters of automobile engines (the starter solenoid engages the pinion with the flywheel).
  • Pinball flippers, coin mechanisms, vending machines, ticket barriers and sorting gates.
  • Printers and dot-matrix print heads; fuel injectors in diesel and petrol engines; shifters in automatic gearboxes.
  • Vibration feeders and small actuators in robots.
Important

A solenoid coil is highly inductive, so a freewheeling diode must be connected across it when it is driven from a transistor. Also, a solenoid rated for intermittent duty will overheat and burn out if it is left energised; the driver software must limit the ON time.

2.Brushed DC Motor

Definition

A brushed DC motor converts direct current electrical energy into mechanical rotation. It works on the principle that a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force (Fleming's left-hand rule), F = B I l.

Construction

  • Field system (stator) — produces the main magnetic flux. It is either a permanent magnet (in small PMDC motors) or an electromagnet made of field poles with field windings. The stationary part carrying the poles is called the yoke or frame, made of cast steel.
  • Armature (rotor) — a cylindrical laminated core of silicon steel stampings with axial slots, mounted on the shaft. Lamination reduces eddy current loss. The slots hold the armature winding, usually of enamelled copper.
  • Commutator — a cylindrical assembly of hard-drawn copper segments insulated from each other by mica, mounted on but insulated from the shaft. It is the mechanical rectifier.
  • Brushes — blocks of carbon or graphite held in brush holders and pressed against the commutator by springs. They carry the current from the stationary supply to the rotating armature.
  • Shaft, bearings and end shields — support the rotor and transmit the torque to the load.
  • Interpoles (in larger machines) — small poles between the main poles that improve commutation and reduce sparking.

Working principle

When DC current enters the armature conductors through the brushes and commutator, each conductor lying in the field experiences a force F = B I l. The forces on the two sides of a coil form a couple that produces a torque and the armature begins to rotate. As the armature turns, the commutator reverses the direction of current in each coil exactly when that coil passes the magnetic neutral axis, so that the torque always acts in the same direction. The armature therefore continues to rotate.

While rotating, the armature conductors cut the main flux, so an EMF is induced in them by Faraday's law. By Lenz's law this EMF opposes the applied voltage and is therefore called the back EMF or counter EMF.

Formula

DC motor equationsEb = (P Φ Z N) / (60 A)V = Eb + Ia Ra so Ia = (V − Eb) / RaT = (P Φ Z Ia) / (2 π A) = k Φ IaN ∝ (V − Ia Ra) / Φwhere P = number of poles, Φ = flux per pole in weber, Z = number of armature conductors, A = number of parallel paths (A = P for lap winding, A = 2 for wave winding), N = speed in rpm, V = supply voltage, Ia = armature current, Ra = armature resistance.

The back EMF is the key to understanding the motor. At the instant of starting, N = 0 so Eb = 0, and the armature current would be V/Ra, which is very large (Ra is only a fraction of an ohm) — this is why a starter resistance is needed for large DC motors and why a small DC motor draws a large stall current. As the motor speeds up, Eb rises and the current falls to the value needed to drive the load. The motor thus adjusts its current automatically to the load.

Important

A DC series motor must never be started without a load. At no load the armature current is small, so the field flux is small, and since N ∝ 1/Φ the speed rises to a dangerously high value that can destroy the rotor by centrifugal force.

Speed control of a DC motor

From N ∝ (V − IaRa)/Φ there are three methods of speed control: armature voltage control (varying V, the most common and efficient method, giving constant torque below base speed), field control (varying Φ, giving speeds above base speed at constant power but reduced torque), and armature resistance control (inserting resistance in series with the armature, which wastes power and is used only for starting).

In mechatronic systems, armature voltage control is implemented by pulse width modulation (PWM). A power switch (MOSFET or IGBT) is turned on and off at a frequency of 1 kHz to 25 kHz, and the average voltage applied to the motor equals the duty cycle times the supply voltage: Vavg = D × VCC. Because the switching frequency is well above the mechanical response of the motor, the armature inductance smooths the current and the motor behaves as if it were fed with a steady DC voltage. PWM control is very efficient because the switch is either fully on (low voltage drop) or fully off (no current).

To reverse the direction of rotation, either the armature connections or the field connections must be reversed (not both). An H-bridge of four switches does this electronically. Integrated H-bridge drivers such as the L293D (600 mA, two bridges), L298N (2 A, two bridges) and TB6612FNG (1.2 A, low loss) are used with microcontrollers.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages: simple and cheap; excellent speed control over a wide range; high starting torque; linear speed-torque characteristic; easy to reverse; control electronics is simple; available in very small sizes.
  • Disadvantages: brushes and commutator wear out and need periodic replacement; sparking at the brushes generates radio interference and is a hazard in explosive atmospheres; limited maximum speed; not suitable for very high power without maintenance; brush friction reduces efficiency; cannot be used in a vacuum or in a sealed environment for very long life.

Because of these disadvantages, brushed motors have been largely replaced by brushless DC motors in high-reliability and high-speed applications, although brushed motors are still widely used in toys, small appliances, automotive accessories and teaching laboratories because of their low cost and simple control.

3.Stepper Motor

Definition

A stepper motor is a brushless DC motor that divides a complete revolution into a large number of equal steps. Each input pulse rotates the shaft by exactly one step angle, so the position is known simply by counting pulses and no feedback encoder is needed. It is therefore a natural actuator for digital control.

Working principle

The stator has a number of salient poles carrying concentrated windings grouped into phases. The rotor carries teeth. When one phase is energised, its poles become electromagnets and the rotor teeth are attracted into alignment with them, because a magnetic circuit always tries to take up the position of minimum reluctance (maximum flux). When the next phase is energised and the first is switched off, the rotor moves to align with the new poles, advancing by one step angle. By energising the phases in a repeating sequence, continuous rotation is obtained. Reversing the order of the sequence reverses the direction of rotation.

Formula

Step angle and resolutionθstep = 360° / (number of steps per revolution)For a motor with Ns stator poles and Nr rotor teeth: θ = 360° (Ns − Nr) / (Ns Nr)Speed in rpm = (60 × f) / (steps per revolution), where f is the pulse frequency in HzLinear travel per pulse = lead of the screw / steps per revolution

Example

Numerical 1. A stepper motor has a step angle of 1.8°. Steps per revolution = 360/1.8 = 200. To turn the shaft through 45° we need 45/1.8 = 25 pulses. If pulses are supplied at 1000 Hz, speed = 60 × 1000/200 = 300 rpm.
Numerical 2. The motor of example 1 drives a lead screw of 5 mm lead. Each pulse moves the nut by 5/200 = 0.025 mm, which is the resolution of the positioning system.
Numerical 3. A VR stepper has 6 stator poles and 4 rotor teeth. Step angle = 360(6−4)/(6×4) = 720/24 = 30°, so 12 steps per revolution.

Types of stepper motor

  • Variable reluctance (VR) — the rotor is a soft iron toothed cylinder with no magnets and no winding. It moves to the position of minimum reluctance. Step angles are typically 15°, 30° or 45°. Cheap and simple, but low torque, no detent torque when unpowered, and prone to resonance.
  • Permanent magnet (PM) — the rotor is a permanent magnet with alternate north and south poles. Step angles are larger, typically 45° or 7.5° per phase, but torque per volume is good and there is detent torque when unpowered. Used in low-cost applications such as printers and instrument drives.
  • Hybrid — combines both ideas: a permanent magnet rotor with two toothed iron cups offset by half a tooth pitch. This gives small step angles of 1.8° or 0.9°, high torque, high resolution and good damping. Almost all modern steppers used in CNC machines, 3D printers and robots are hybrid.
  • Unipolar versus bipolar winding — a unipolar motor has a centre tap on each phase (5 or 6 wires) and current always flows in the same direction in each half winding; it is driven simply by a transistor array such as the ULN2003, but only half the winding is used at a time so torque is lower. A bipolar motor has 4 wires and needs an H-bridge per phase (L293D, A4988, DRV8825) but uses the whole winding and gives more torque.

Excitation modes

  • One-phase-on (wave drive) — only one phase is energised at a time. Lowest power consumption and lowest torque; the rotor tends to oscillate about the detent position.
  • Two-phase-on (full step) — two adjacent phases are energised together, so the rotor settles midway between them. Torque is about 40 % higher than wave drive and the motion is smoother. Same step angle as wave drive.
  • Half stepping — alternates between one-phase-on and two-phase-on in an 8-step sequence. The step angle is halved (1.8° becomes 0.9°), the resolution doubles, and the torque ripple is reduced.
  • Microstepping — the currents in the two phases are varied sinusoidally in small increments, so the rotor can be positioned in fractions of a full step. Typical microstepping is 1/8, 1/16 or 1/32 of a step. This greatly reduces noise, vibration and resonance, but the holding torque per microstep is very small and positional accuracy does not improve proportionally.

Torque-speed characteristic and important terms

The torque of a stepper motor is highest at standstill (holding torque) and falls rapidly as the stepping rate increases, because the winding inductance limits how quickly the current can build up in each phase. The pull-in torque is the maximum torque at which the motor can start and stop instantly with a load attached, without losing a step; the region below the pull-in curve is the start/stop region. Above this, the motor must be ramped up in speed gradually. The pull-out torque is the maximum torque the motor can develop at a given speed once it is already running and synchronised; the region between the pull-in and pull-out curves is the slewing region, where the motor can run but cannot start.

  • Holding torque — torque required to move the shaft one full step when the motor is energised but stationary.
  • Detent (residual) torque — the torque present when the motor is unpowered, due to the permanent magnets.
  • Pull-in / pull-out torque — as described above.
  • Step angle accuracy — typically ±3 % to ±5 % of a step, and non-cumulative, so errors do not build up.
  • Resonance — at certain step rates (usually 100 to 300 steps/s) the rotor oscillates and may lose synchronism. It is reduced by microstepping, by mechanical damping, by a smaller step angle, or by accelerating quickly through the resonant band.
  • Maximum start frequency and slew frequency — the highest pulse rates at which the motor can start and run respectively.

Advantages, disadvantages and applications

  • Advantages: open loop position control — no encoder or feedback needed, so the system is simple and cheap; precise and repeatable positioning, with non-cumulative error; excellent low-speed torque; holds position when energised; easy to interface to a microcontroller; very reliable with no brushes; fast response to start, stop and reverse; wide range of speed controlled simply by pulse frequency.
  • Disadvantages: torque falls sharply with speed, so it is not suitable for high-speed drives; low efficiency — it draws full current even when stationary; can lose steps if overloaded, and because there is no feedback the controller does not know; resonance and vibration at certain speeds; noisy; low torque-to-inertia ratio; the motor and driver get warm; cannot correct for an externally applied disturbance.
  • Applications: CNC machine tools, 3D printers, laser cutters and engravers; computer peripherals such as floppy drives, printers, plotters and scanners; robotics for joint positioning and gripper drives; textile machinery; medical equipment such as syringe pumps, analysers and scanning stages; camera lens focusing and shutter control; telescope and satellite antenna positioning; automated valves and dampers; pick-and-place machines; indexing tables and rotary conveyors; digital watches and instrument drives.

★Summary

  • A solenoid converts electrical energy into a short linear stroke; the force is proportional to (NI)²/g², so it is greatest at the end of the stroke when the air gap is smallest.
  • Solenoid types include linear pull, push, rotary, latching, proportional and voice-coil; duty cycle rating must be respected or the coil burns out.
  • A brushed DC motor consists of a field system, a laminated armature, a commutator and carbon brushes. Torque T = kΦIa, back EMF Eb = V − IaRa, and N ∝ (V − IaRa)/Φ.
  • Back EMF limits the armature current; at standstill Eb = 0 so the starting current is very high.
  • Shunt motors run at nearly constant speed, series motors have very high starting torque but must never run unloaded, and PMDC motors have a linear speed-torque characteristic.
  • Speed is controlled by armature voltage using PWM, where Vavg = D × VCC; an H-bridge reverses the direction.
  • A stepper motor moves one exact step angle per input pulse, so position is obtained by counting pulses with no feedback. Step angle = 360° / steps per revolution.
  • Types are variable reluctance, permanent magnet and hybrid; hybrid gives 1.8° or 0.9° steps with high torque and is the most common.
  • Excitation modes are wave (one phase on), full step (two phases on), half stepping and microstepping; microstepping reduces noise and resonance.
  • Stepper motors give simple, cheap, repeatable open loop positioning but lose torque rapidly with speed, are inefficient, may lose steps and can resonate.
