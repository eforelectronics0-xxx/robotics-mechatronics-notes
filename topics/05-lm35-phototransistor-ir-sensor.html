05LM35 Temperature Sensor, Phototransistor and IR Sensor

Three easy-to-use sensors for the laboratory and for projects: the LM35 analogue temperature sensor with its 10 mV per degree output, the phototransistor for light detection, and the reflective IR sensor module used for obstacle avoidance and line following.

On this page

  1. LM35 Temperature Sensor
  2. Phototransistor
  3. IR Sensor (Infrared Reflective / Obstacle Sensor)

1.LM35 Temperature Sensor

Definition

The LM35 is a precision integrated-circuit temperature sensor whose output voltage is directly proportional to the Celsius temperature. Unlike a thermocouple or a thermistor, it needs no external calibration, no linearisation network and no cold-junction compensation.

Formula

LM35 transfer equationVout = 10 mV / °C × TCExamples: 0 °C → 0 mV; 25 °C → 250 mV; 50 °C → 500 mV; 100 °C → 1.0 V; 150 °C → 1.5 V.

Salient features

  • Calibrated directly in degrees Celsius, so no subtraction of 2.73 V is needed as with the LM335 (which is calibrated in kelvin).
  • Linear scale factor of +10.0 mV/°C.
  • Accuracy guaranteed to ±0.5 °C at 25 °C, and typically ±0.25 °C over the full range for the LM35/LM35A grade.
  • Operating range −55 °C to +150 °C for the standard version (LM35C, LM35CA: −40 °C to +110 °C).
  • Works from a single supply of 4 V to 30 V; low cost.
  • Very low self-heating, less than 0.1 °C in still air, because it draws only about 60 µA.
  • Low output impedance of about 0.1 Ω for a 1 mA load, so long cables can be used.
  • Output can go down to 0 V, which suits single-supply circuits.

Working and interfacing

Inside the IC a band-gap reference and a differential amplifier produce a current that is proportional to absolute temperature; this is converted into a voltage referred to the Celsius scale. From the user's point of view the device behaves as a small voltage source with a very low series resistance.

For interfacing with a microcontroller, pin 1 is connected to +5 V, pin 3 to ground, and pin 2 to an analogue input through a small series resistor if desired. A 0.1 µF ceramic capacitor between the supply pin and ground, placed as close to the IC as possible, removes any high-frequency noise. For long cable runs a capacitor of 1 µF at the sensor end is recommended. If the temperature can fall below 0 °C, the ground pin must be raised above true ground (for example by connecting it to a resistor to a negative supply or to a 1.5 V reference), because a single positive supply cannot produce a negative output voltage.

Note

Important caution: the LM35 pin-out is different from that of many other TO-92 devices such as the transistor BC547 or the LM335. Connecting the supply to the wrong pin will destroy the IC immediately. Always verify pin 1 by holding the flat face towards you and reading from left to right.

Reading the temperature in software

With a 10-bit ADC and a 5 V reference, each ADC count represents 5/1024 = 4.88 mV. The temperature is therefore

T (°C) = (ADC value × 4.88 mV) / 10 mV = ADC value × 0.488
Example

Numerical. The ADC reads 512. Voltage = 512 × 4.88 mV = 2.50 V. Temperature = 2.50 / 0.010 = 250 °C? No — 2.50 V would mean 250 °C which is outside the LM35 range, so the reading indicates a fault (perhaps the supply pin and output pin are swapped). A realistic reading is 256, giving 256 × 4.88 = 1.25 V and T = 125 °C. This shows why the range of the sensor must always be checked against the reading. If a higher resolution is required, a 3.3 V reference or a pre-amplifier of gain 2 to 5 can be used, since the LM35 output is small.

Applications

The LM35 is used in digital thermometers, in temperature control of incubators, ovens, greenhouses and aquariums, in battery chargers and power supply thermal protection, in air conditioners and refrigerators, in engine and gearbox oil temperature monitoring, in cold storage and in weather stations. Because it is cheap, linear and accurate, it is the first choice for student projects on temperature measurement and control.

2.Phototransistor

Definition

A phototransistor is a bipolar junction transistor whose base-collector junction is exposed to light through a transparent window or a lens. Light falling on the junction generates electron-hole pairs, producing a base current which is amplified by the transistor's current gain β. The result is a collector current that is proportional to the intensity of the incident light, but much larger than that of a photodiode for the same illumination.

Construction and types

  • NPN phototransistor — the most common type; the collector is positive with respect to the emitter.
  • PNP phototransistor — less common; used where the load must be connected to ground.
  • Photodarlington — two transistors connected as a Darlington pair, giving a current gain of 10 000 or more. Much more sensitive but slower.
  • Base-lead version — has an electrical base connection as well, allowing the sensitivity and the operating point to be set by a bias resistor. Most phototransistors have only two leads, collector and emitter.

Working principle

In the dark, only a tiny leakage current flows, so the transistor is effectively OFF. When light of suitable wavelength (usually 400 nm to 1100 nm, peak near 850 nm to 940 nm) strikes the base-collector depletion region, photons with energy greater than the band gap create electron-hole pairs. These carriers are swept across the junction and act as a base current IB. The transistor amplifies this by β, so the collector current is

IC = β × IB(photo)

Since IB(photo) is proportional to the light intensity, the collector current is also proportional to intensity. The device therefore works as a light-controlled current source.

Load resistor circuit and design

The phototransistor is connected in a common-emitter arrangement with a load resistor RL between the collector and the positive supply. The output voltage is taken from the collector.

Vout = VCC − IC × RL

In darkness, IC is nearly zero, so Vout ≈ VCC (HIGH). In bright light, IC is large, the voltage drop across RL is large, and Vout falls towards zero (LOW). The circuit is therefore inverting.

The value of RL is a compromise. A large RL (say 100 kΩ) gives a large output swing and high sensitivity but makes the circuit slow, because the junction capacitance has to charge through the large resistance. A small RL (say 1 kΩ) is fast but gives a small output. Values between 1 kΩ and 47 kΩ are typical; 10 kΩ is a good starting point. The output should be buffered or fed to a comparator or a Schmitt trigger before being used as a digital signal.

Note

Phototransistors respond mainly to infrared as well as visible light. If only visible light should be detected, a filter must be used; if infrared is being detected, a plastic package with a clear (not black) lens or an IR-pass filter gives the best result.

Applications

  • Opto-couplers / opto-isolators (such as the 4N35 or PC817) in which an LED and a phototransistor are in one package, providing electrical isolation between two circuits.
  • Optical encoders and slotted opto-switches for measuring shaft speed and position.
  • Object detection and counting on conveyors, and paper-end detection in printers.
  • Line following and obstacle avoidance in mobile robots (usually as part of an IR module).
  • Light meters, automatic brightness control of displays, and safety light curtains.
  • Remote controls (in the form of IR receiver modules) and fibre optic links.

3.IR Sensor (Infrared Reflective / Obstacle Sensor)

An IR sensor in mechatronics practice usually means a module that contains an infrared LED as the transmitter and a phototransistor or photodiode as the receiver, working at a wavelength of about 940 nm, which is invisible to the human eye. Such modules are the workhorses of line-following and obstacle-avoidance robots.

Types of IR sensing arrangement

  • Reflective type — the transmitter and receiver are side by side, facing the same direction. Light reflected from a nearby object returns to the receiver. Used for obstacle detection, line following and object counting.
  • Transmissive / slotted type — the transmitter and receiver face each other across a gap or slot. An object passing through the slot interrupts the beam. Used in encoders, tachometers and end-stop detection. Examples: MOC7811, ITR9606.
  • Diffuse reflective with background suppression — uses triangulation to detect an object only within a set distance range.
  • IR receiver module for remote control — such as the TSOP1738, which contains a photodiode, an amplifier, a band-pass filter tuned to 38 kHz and a demodulator, and gives a digital output for the coded signals from a TV remote.

Working principle of the reflective type

  1. The IR LED is driven by a series current-limiting resistor (typically 220 Ω to 330 Ω) from a 5 V supply and emits an infrared beam.
  2. If an object is within the sensing range, part of the beam is reflected back. A white or light-coloured surface reflects much more than a black surface, which is why a line-following robot can distinguish white paper from a black line.
  3. The reflected light falls on the phototransistor, which conducts more heavily, pulling its collector voltage down.
  4. This voltage is compared with a reference voltage set by a preset (trimmer potentiometer) in a comparator such as the LM358 or LM393.
  5. When the received light exceeds the threshold, the comparator output changes state, giving a clean digital HIGH or LOW to the microcontroller.
  6. An on-board LED usually lights to indicate detection, which is very useful for alignment and testing.

Practical notes and common modules

The detection range of a reflective IR module is short, usually 2 mm to 30 mm, and is adjusted by the on-board preset. It works best in the shade or indoors; strong ambient sunlight contains a lot of infrared and can saturate the receiver, which is why outdoor robots often modulate the IR LED with a 38 kHz carrier and use a matched TSOP receiver. The supply is normally 3.3 V or 5 V, the output is a digital level (some modules give an open-collector output that needs a pull-up), and the current consumption is 15 mA to 25 mA per module.

Applications

  • Line following robots — an array of reflective IR sensors detects a black line on a white surface.
  • Obstacle avoidance in mobile robots, vacuum cleaners and automated guided vehicles.
  • Object counting and part presence detection on production lines.
  • Edge detection — preventing a robot from falling off a table.
  • Speed and position measurement using a slotted disc and a transmissive sensor.
  • Touchless switches, paper detection in printers, and coin validators.
  • Remote control receivers and IR data links between devices.
  • Flame and heat source detection in fire alarms.
Important

An IR LED must always be used with a series current-limiting resistor. Connecting it directly across a 5 V supply will burn it out within seconds. A typical safe current is 10 mA to 20 mA, giving R = (5 − 1.2) / 0.015 ≈ 250 Ω, so 220 Ω or 330 Ω is used.

★Summary

  • The LM35 gives an output of exactly 10 mV per degree Celsius, starting from 0 mV at 0 °C, and needs no calibration or linearisation.
  • LM35 pins in TO-92 with the flat face towards you are 1 = +VS, 2 = Vout, 3 = GND. Supply is 4 V to 30 V and the current drawn is only about 60 µA.
  • With a 10-bit ADC and 5 V reference, T = ADC value × 0.488 °C. A decoupling capacitor of 0.1 µF should be placed close to the supply pin.
  • A phototransistor is a BJT whose base-collector junction is light sensitive; light generates a base current that is amplified by β, so it is far more sensitive than a photodiode but slower than a photodiode and faster than an LDR.
  • In a common-emitter phototransistor circuit Vout = VCC − ICRL; the output is HIGH in darkness and LOW in light. RL of 10 kΩ is a good compromise between sensitivity and speed.
  • An IR sensor module pairs a 940 nm IR LED with a phototransistor and a comparator, and works in reflective or transmissive mode.
  • Reflective IR range is a few millimetres to a few centimetres and depends strongly on surface colour; sunlight can saturate the receiver, so modulation at 38 kHz is used outdoors.
  • Always use a series resistor with an IR LED; 220 Ω to 330 Ω at 5 V is normal.
