19Classification by Control Method and Major Components of a Robot

How robots are classified by the way their motion is controlled, from simple manual handling to intelligent sensor-driven autonomy, and a detailed description of every major component of a robot.

On this page

  1. Classification of Robots by Control Method
  2. Major Components of a Robot

1.Classification of Robots by Control Method

The control method describes how the robot's motion is commanded and how much information the controller has about the actual result. Six levels are recognised, in increasing order of sophistication.

1. Manual control (limited sequence robot)

The operator directly controls each motion using a joystick, pendant or buttons. There is no stored program; the robot does only what the operator commands at that instant. Motion may be limited by mechanical stops. This is used for simple handling tasks, for set-up and for teaching. Examples: a teleoperator handling radioactive material, a simple two-axis pick-and-place unit, a joystick-controlled wheelchair.

2. Point-to-point (PTP) control

Only the end points of a motion are programmed. The robot moves from point A to point B, and the controller ensures that it arrives at B, but the path taken between A and B is not controlled. Each joint moves at its own maximum speed, so the tool follows an unpredictable curved path. All the joints normally start and stop together.

  • Advantages: simplest to program; fastest point-to-point motion because all joints move simultaneously at full speed; only a few points need to be stored.
  • Disadvantages: the path is unknown, so the tool may collide with an obstacle; the speed along the path is not controlled; not suitable for any process where the trajectory matters.
  • Applications: spot welding (the electrode must reach a point, and the path does not matter), pick and place, machine loading and unloading, drilling, riveting, component insertion, palletising.

3. Continuous path (CP) or controlled path control

The entire path between points is controlled, and so is the speed along it. The controller continuously interpolates the position of the tool and corrects any deviation. To program such a path, either a very large number of closely spaced points must be taught, or a mathematical description of the path (a line, an arc, a circle) is given and the controller generates the intermediate points itself.

  • Advantages: the tool follows exactly the required trajectory at the required speed, which is essential for many processes; safe because the path is known and predictable.
  • Disadvantages: more difficult to program; requires more memory and more computing power; motion is slower than PTP because the path must be followed exactly.
  • Applications: arc welding, spray painting, adhesive sealing, gluing, grinding, polishing, deburring, flame cutting, laser cutting, waterjet cutting, milling and all tasks where the tool must follow a defined contour.

4. Playback (teach and repeat) control

The operator moves the robot through the desired path by jogging it with a teach pendant, or by physically guiding the arm (this is called lead-through or walk-through programming). At each significant position the operator presses a button and the controller records the joint angles (and sometimes the speed) into memory. Afterwards the robot repeats the recorded sequence exactly, cycle after cycle.

Playback is the most common method of programming industrial robots because it needs no knowledge of mathematics or of programming languages. Modern controllers combine playback with the ability to edit the recorded points, to insert logic, to add conditional branches, and to modify positions arithmetically.

5. Numerical control (CNC-type) control

The robot is programmed by writing a part program in a text-based language, without touching the robot at all. The controller reads the program, performs interpolation, and drives the axes. Languages include the robot-specific ones such as VAL (Unimation), KAREL (FANUC), RAPID (ABB), KRL (KUKA), INFORM (Yaskawa), MotoPlus, and the general G-code used by CNC machines and 3D printers. Programming can be done off-line on a computer with a 3D simulation of the cell, which avoids stopping production.

  • Advantages: no need to have the robot available while programming; easy to store, copy, edit and version-control; can be generated automatically from a CAD model; simulation detects collisions before any hardware is at risk.
  • Disadvantages: requires skilled programmers; the simulated positions may not exactly match the real machine, so a small amount of touch-up teaching is usually still needed.

6. Intelligent (adaptive / sensor-based) control

The highest level. The robot is given a task rather than a motion, and it works out how to perform it using information from its own sensors. It can recognise objects with a camera, measure forces with a wrist sensor, avoid obstacles it has not been told about, adjust its grip, recover from errors and even learn from experience using machine learning. Examples: vision-guided picking of randomly oriented parts, autonomous mobile robots navigating a warehouse, surgical robots that compensate for a patient's breathing, robotic vacuum cleaners mapping a room, and agricultural robots identifying and picking ripe fruit.

2.Major Components of a Robot

A robot is a mechatronic system, and its components can be grouped exactly as in Topic 1: mechanical structure, actuators, sensors, controller, power supply and end effector.

1. The manipulator (arm and body)

The manipulator is the mechanical arm itself. It consists of a series of rigid bodies called links connected by joints. The joints are driven by actuators. The link fixed to the floor is the base; the moving assembly attached to it is the arm; the assembly at the end of the arm that orients the tool is the wrist. The joint nearest the base is called the waist or shoulder and carries the largest load, so it is the strongest and largest. Each joint has mechanical limits (hard stops) and usually a brake to hold position when power is removed.

Links are made of cast or machined aluminium alloy for light weight and rigidity, or of steel where strength is needed, or of carbon fibre composite in high-speed robots to reduce inertia. The links are hollow wherever possible, so that cables and hoses can be routed inside them.

2. Joints and actuators

Each joint is driven by an actuator. The three types of actuator used in robots are:

  • Electric — by far the most common. Brushless DC (BLDC) or AC permanent magnet synchronous servo motors with harmonic or planetary gear reducers and absolute encoders. Clean, quiet, accurate, easy to control, and efficient. Used in almost all modern industrial and service robots.
  • Hydraulic — a hydraulic cylinder or a hydraulic motor driven by a servo valve. Gives an extremely high force-to-weight ratio and is very stiff, so it is used for very heavy payloads (over 500 kg), for large foundry and forging robots, and historically for walking robots. It needs a hydraulic power pack, is noisy, can leak oil, and is less precise.
  • Pneumatic — uses compressed air cylinders. Very cheap, fast, clean and safe in explosive atmospheres, but air is compressible so the position cannot be controlled precisely; only two positions (fully extended and fully retracted) are practical. Used for simple grippers and for low-cost pick-and-place robots.

The transmission between the motor and the joint is usually a harmonic drive (strain wave gear) or a cycloidal / RV reducer, because these give very high reduction ratios with almost zero backlash in a compact package. This is discussed in detail in Topic 24.

3. Sensors

Robot sensors are divided into two groups.

  • Internal (proprioceptive) sensors — measure the internal state of the robot itself: joint position and velocity (encoders, resolvers, potentiometers), motor current and torque, brake status, and temperature. These close the servo position loops and are essential for the robot to work at all.
  • External (exteroceptive) sensors — measure the robot's relationship with its environment: vision (2D and 3D cameras) to locate parts, force and torque sensors at the wrist to control contact force, tactile sensors on the gripper fingers to control grip and to detect slip, proximity sensors to detect the presence of a part, range finders (ultrasonic, laser, LiDAR) for mobile robot navigation, and safety scanners to detect people.

4. End effector

The end effector is the device mounted on the wrist that actually performs the task. It is not part of the robot itself and is changed according to the job. There are two broad categories: grippers, which hold and move a workpiece, and tools, which perform an operation such as welding, painting, cutting or drilling. A tool changer allows one robot to switch automatically between several end effectors. End effectors are covered in detail in Topic 29.

5. Controller

The controller is a dedicated industrial computer that houses the processor, the memory, the servo drives, the input/output interfaces and the safety circuits. Its tasks are:

  • to store the robot program and the taught positions;
  • to execute the program, including logic, branching, loops and calls to subroutines;
  • to perform the kinematic calculations — converting the desired tool position into the joint angles required (inverse kinematics) and the reverse (forward kinematics);
  • to generate the trajectory, interpolating between taught points at the commanded speed;
  • to run the servo control loops for each joint, typically at 1 kHz or higher, using PID or computed-torque control;
  • to handle communication with the teach pendant, with other machines, with vision systems and with the factory network;
  • to monitor all safety devices and to stop the robot safely in an emergency;
  • to log faults and to provide diagnostics.

Modern controllers use a multi-processor or multi-core architecture, with one processor for the user program and trajectory planning and a separate digital signal processor for each joint's servo loop.

6. Teach pendant and programming devices

The teach pendant is a hand-held terminal, connected by a cable, with a display, a joystick or axis keys, an enabling (dead-man) switch and an emergency stop button. It is used to jog the robot, to record positions, to write and edit programs, and to set parameters. The enabling switch is a three-position device: the robot can move only when the switch is held in the middle position, so a startled operator who either lets go or squeezes hard will stop the machine. This is a legal safety requirement.

7. Power supply

Industrial robots are normally fed from a three-phase 400 V, 50 Hz supply, which is rectified to a DC bus (about 540 V to 650 V) and then inverted for each motor. A braking resistor absorbs the energy returned by the motors during deceleration, or a regenerative unit feeds it back to the mains. Small and mobile robots use 24 V DC or batteries. Hydraulic robots additionally need a hydraulic power pack with a pump, a reservoir, filters, a cooler and accumulators.

8. Safety equipment

  • Emergency stop buttons at the pendant and on the cell.
  • Safety fences, interlocked gates and light curtains so that a person cannot enter the cell while the robot is moving.
  • Safety scanners and laser area monitors around the robot.
  • Safe speed and safe position monitoring in the controller.
  • Mechanical hard stops and brakes that hold the arm when power is lost.
  • Force and power limiting in collaborative robots, with rounded, padded surfaces.

★Summary

  • Robots are classified by control method into manual, point-to-point, continuous path, playback, numerical control and intelligent control.
  • Point-to-point control programs only the end points; the path between them is uncontrolled. It is fastest and simplest, and suits spot welding and pick and place.
  • Continuous path control commands the whole trajectory and the speed along it, which is essential for arc welding, painting, grinding and cutting.
  • Playback programming records joint positions while the operator jogs or guides the robot; it needs no programming skill.
  • Numerical and off-line programming uses text languages such as RAPID, KAREL, KRL and VAL, and allows simulation before production.
  • Intelligent control uses sensors, vision and artificial intelligence to decide the actions needed to complete a task.
  • The major components of a robot are the manipulator (links and joints), the actuators, the transmission, internal and external sensors, the end effector, the controller, the teach pendant, the power supply and the safety equipment.
  • Electric actuators dominate; hydraulic ones give the highest force; pneumatic ones are cheapest but only give two positions.
  • Internal sensors close the joint servo loops; external sensors close the task loops.
  • The teach pendant has an enabling (dead-man) switch that must be held in the middle position for the robot to move.
