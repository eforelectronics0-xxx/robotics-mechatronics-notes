11AC Motor, BLDC Motor and PMDC Motor

The induction motor that runs industry, the brushless DC motor that combines the simplicity of DC control with the reliability of AC construction, and the small permanent magnet DC motor found in every toy and automobile accessory.

On this page

  1. AC Motor
  2. BLDC Motor (Brushless DC Motor)
  3. PMDC Motor (Permanent Magnet DC Motor)

1.AC Motor

Alternating current motors are used wherever the supply is AC (230 V single-phase or 400 V three-phase, 50 Hz in India) and where large power, ruggedness and low maintenance are required. The most important type by far is the three-phase squirrel cage induction motor, which accounts for the great majority of all industrial drives. Other types are the synchronous motor, the single-phase induction motor and the universal motor.

Construction of a three-phase induction motor

  • Stator — a cylindrical frame (yoke) of cast iron or rolled steel, carrying a laminated core of silicon steel stampings with semi-closed slots. Three-phase windings are placed in the slots, displaced by 120 electrical degrees and connected either in star or delta. The laminations are insulated from each other by a thin varnish to reduce eddy current loss.
  • Rotor — two types exist. The squirrel cage rotor consists of a laminated cylinder with axial slots containing solid aluminium or copper bars, all short-circuited at both ends by end rings. It looks like a squirrel cage, hence the name. It is extremely rugged, cheap and needs no maintenance because there are no brushes, no slip rings and no commutator. About 90 % of induction motors are of this type. The slip ring (wound) rotor has a three-phase winding connected to three slip rings, allowing external resistance to be inserted in the rotor circuit for higher starting torque and lower starting current.
  • End shields, bearings, shaft and fan — support the rotor, and the shaft-mounted fan provides cooling.

Working principle

When a balanced three-phase supply is given to the stator windings, a rotating magnetic field (RMF) of constant magnitude is produced, which revolves at the synchronous speed Ns. This rotating field cuts the stationary rotor conductors. Because the rotor bars form a closed circuit, an EMF is induced in them by electromagnetic induction and a current flows. The current-carrying rotor conductors, lying in the rotating field, experience a force, and by Lenz's law the rotor begins to turn in the same direction as the rotating field, trying to catch up with it.

The rotor can never reach the synchronous speed. If it did, there would be no relative motion between the field and the rotor, hence no induced EMF, no rotor current and no torque. The rotor therefore always runs slightly slower than the field, and the difference is called the slip.

Formula

Induction motor relationsNs = 120 f / P (rpm)slip s = (Ns − Nr) / Ns (as a fraction, or × 100 for percentage)rotor frequency fr = s × frotor speed Nr = Ns (1 − s)where f = supply frequency in Hz, P = number of stator poles, Nr = actual rotor speed in rpm. At standstill s = 1 and fr = f; at synchronous speed s = 0; at full load s is typically 2 % to 5 %.

Example

Numerical. A 4-pole, 3-phase, 50 Hz induction motor has a full-load slip of 4 %. Find the synchronous speed, the rotor speed and the rotor frequency. Ns = 120 × 50/4 = 1500 rpm. Nr = 1500 (1 − 0.04) = 1440 rpm. fr = 0.04 × 50 = 2 Hz.

Starting and speed control

At starting the slip is 1, the rotor frequency equals the supply frequency, and the motor draws 5 to 8 times its full-load current. Direct-on-line starting is used only for small motors; larger motors use star-delta starting, an auto-transformer starter, a soft starter (back-to-back thyristors that ramp the voltage up), or slip-ring motors with rotor resistance starters.

The speed of an induction motor can be controlled by: changing the number of poles (discrete speeds only), changing the supply frequency (the best method, using a variable frequency drive or VFD, which keeps V/f constant so that the flux stays constant), changing the supply voltage (limited range), inserting rotor resistance (slip-ring motors only), and by cascading two motors. Modern mechatronic systems almost always use a VFD.

Single-phase induction motor

A single-phase supply produces a pulsating field, not a rotating one, so a single-phase induction motor is not self-starting. Auxiliary means are used to create a rotating field at start: split-phase (a high-resistance starting winding), capacitor-start (a capacitor in series with the starting winding, giving higher starting torque), capacitor-start capacitor-run, permanent split capacitor (PSC) and shaded pole (a copper shading ring, used for very small loads such as fans). A centrifugal switch disconnects the starting winding once the motor reaches about 75 % of synchronous speed.

Synchronous motor and universal motor

A synchronous motor runs at exactly the synchronous speed regardless of load. Its rotor is separately excited by DC through slip rings or carries permanent magnets, and it locks into step with the rotating field. It is used where constant speed is essential and also for power factor improvement. A universal motor is essentially a DC series motor built with a laminated field so that it can run on AC as well; it reaches very high speeds (up to 20 000 rpm) and is used in mixers, grinders, drills, vacuum cleaners and hair dryers.

2.BLDC Motor (Brushless DC Motor)

Definition

A brushless DC motor is a permanent magnet synchronous motor with a trapezoidal back EMF waveform, driven by a DC supply through an electronic inverter that performs the commutation instead of a mechanical commutator and brushes. In effect, the BLDC motor is a DC motor turned inside out: the magnets are on the rotor and the windings are on the stator.

Construction

  • Stator — made of laminated steel with three-phase windings distributed in the slots, exactly like an AC motor. Most BLDC motors have three phases in a star (wye) connection. Because the windings are stationary, heat is easily conducted away and the current does not have to pass through sliding contacts.
  • Rotor — carries permanent magnets of sintered neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) or ferrite, giving a strong field. The number of poles may be 2, 4, 6 or more; more poles mean lower speed and higher torque. Some BLDC motors have the magnets on the outside rotating around the stator (outrunner, used in drones for high torque) and some have them inside (inrunner, used for high speed).
  • Position sensor — three Hall effect sensors mounted on the stator at 120 electrical degrees tell the controller the rotor position within one electrical cycle. Alternatively, the position can be estimated without sensors (sensorless control) by measuring the back EMF of the floating third phase.
  • Drive electronics — a three-phase inverter bridge of six power MOSFETs (three half bridges), a controller (microcontroller or DSP) that generates the six-step commutation pattern, and often a gate driver IC. Integrated driver ICs such as the L6234, DRV10983 or MP6540 are available.

Working principle

The stator windings are energised in a sequence that produces a rotating magnetic field in discrete 60 electrical degree steps. In each step, exactly two phases conduct (one connected to the positive rail and one to the negative) while the third phase is left floating. The interaction between this stator field and the permanent magnet field of the rotor produces a torque, and the rotor turns towards alignment. Just before it aligns, the Hall sensors signal the change of position and the controller switches to the next step, so the stator field jumps ahead by 60 degrees and the rotor follows again. Six such steps complete one electrical cycle. This is called six-step or 120-degree trapezoidal commutation.

Speed is controlled by varying the DC link voltage or, more commonly, by applying PWM to the conducting switches. Torque is proportional to the current. Reversal is achieved by reversing the order of the commutation sequence.

N = (120 × fe) / P , where fe = electrical commutation frequency in Hz and P = number of poles

Advantages, disadvantages and applications

  • Advantages: no brushes, so no wear, no sparking and very long life; higher efficiency and power density; excellent heat dissipation because the windings are on the stator; very high speed capability; precise speed and torque control; low electromagnetic interference; quiet operation; can operate in explosive or clean environments; rotor inertia is low so dynamic response is fast.
  • Disadvantages: needs an electronic controller and position sensing, so the overall system is costlier and more complex; the controller can fail; permanent magnets can be demagnetised by excessive current or high temperature, and NdFeB magnets are expensive; torque ripple exists because of the six-step commutation; sensorless starting from rest is difficult.
  • Applications: computer cooling fans and hard disc spindle drives; electric bicycles, scooters and vehicles; drones and model aircraft; washing machines and air conditioners (direct drive); medical equipment such as ventilators, centrifuges and surgical tools; CNC spindles; robotic joints and actuators; blowers and pumps; automotive power steering, fuel pumps and electric water pumps.

3.PMDC Motor (Permanent Magnet DC Motor)

Definition

A PMDC motor is a brushed DC motor in which the stator field is produced by permanent magnets instead of a field winding. It is the smallest, simplest and cheapest of all DC motors and is used wherever fractional-horsepower power and simple speed control are required.

Construction

  • Stator — a steel cylindrical yoke to which two (or more) curved permanent magnet poles are bonded or clipped. Ferrite (ceramic) magnets are used for low-cost motors and NdFeB or samarium-cobalt for high-performance ones. The magnets are already magnetised, so the motor needs no field supply at all.
  • Rotor (armature) — a laminated slotted iron core on the shaft, carrying a multi-coil copper winding, exactly as in any DC motor.
  • Commutator and brushes — a segmented copper commutator and two carbon or precious-metal brushes reverse the armature current twice per revolution.
  • Bearings and shaft — sintered bronze bushes for small motors, ball bearings for larger or higher-speed ones.

Working principle

The working is identical to that of any DC motor. When DC voltage is applied to the armature through the brushes and commutator, current flows in the armature conductors. These conductors lie in the fixed field of the permanent magnets and experience a force F = B I l. The forces on the two sides of each coil produce a torque that turns the rotor, and the commutator reverses the coil current every half revolution so that the torque remains in one direction.

Because the field flux Φ is constant (fixed by the magnets and not adjustable), the equations simplify. Torque is directly proportional to armature current, back EMF is directly proportional to speed, and the speed-torque characteristic is a straight line.

Formula

PMDC equationsT = Kt Ia (Kt = torque constant in N m/A)Eb = Ke ω (Ke = back EMF constant in V/(rad/s))V = Eb + Ia Ra = Ke ω + Ia Raω = (V − Ia Ra) / Ke = V/Ke − (Ra / KeKt) TIn SI units Kt = Ke. The no-load speed is V/Ke and the stall torque is KtV/Ra. The slope of the speed-torque line is fixed by the armature resistance.

Example

Numerical. A PMDC motor has Kt = Ke = 0.02 N m/A (equivalently V per rad/s), Ra = 2 Ω and is supplied with 12 V. No-load speed = V/Ke = 12/0.02 = 600 rad/s = 600 × 60/(2π) = 5730 rpm. Stall current = V/Ra = 6 A, so stall torque = 0.02 × 6 = 0.12 N m. If the load torque is 0.04 N m, armature current = 0.04/0.02 = 2 A, Eb = 12 − 2×2 = 8 V, and speed = 8/0.02 = 400 rad/s = 3820 rpm.

Characteristics, advantages, disadvantages and applications

  • Characteristics: linear speed-torque curve; speed falls steadily as load torque increases; high starting torque; very simple control — speed is nearly proportional to the applied voltage, so PWM control is easy; reversing needs only reversal of the supply polarity (or an H-bridge).
  • Advantages: simple, compact, light and cheap; no field winding so no field copper loss, giving higher efficiency for its size; linear and predictable characteristics; easy to control; good torque at low speed; available in very small sizes (a few millimetres diameter).
  • Disadvantages: brushes and commutator wear out, so life is limited (typically 1000 to 5000 hours) and sparking causes radio interference; the field flux cannot be varied, so field weakening for speeds above base speed is not possible; the magnets can be demagnetised by excessive armature current (a stall), by mechanical shock or by high temperature, and cannot be restored; maximum power is limited to a few hundred watts; not suitable for hazardous atmospheres because of sparking.
  • Applications: toys, models and hobby robotics; automobile accessories such as windshield wipers, power windows, seat adjustment, blower fans, mirror adjustment and fuel pumps; domestic appliances such as mixers, shavers, toothbrushes and small fans; computer peripherals such as CD/DVD tray drives and printers; battery-operated tools; small servo systems and instrument drives; medical devices; vending machines and coin-operated equipment.
Note

PMDC motors are very often combined with a gearbox to make a geared DC motor. A gear reduction of 30:1 or 100:1 lowers the speed and multiplies the torque by the same factor, which is exactly what a robot wheel or joint needs. Encoder-equipped versions are called gear motors with feedback and form the basis of many servo drives.

★Summary

  • A three-phase induction motor has a laminated stator with three-phase windings and a squirrel cage or slip-ring rotor. The rotating magnetic field induces rotor currents, which produce torque.
  • Ns = 120f/P; slip s = (Ns − Nr)/Ns; rotor frequency fr = sf. The rotor can never reach synchronous speed.
  • Induction motors draw 5 to 8 times full-load current at starting; star-delta, soft starters and VFDs are used to limit it. A VFD keeps V/f constant to control speed.
  • A single-phase induction motor is not self-starting and needs a starting winding with a capacitor or a shading ring.
  • A BLDC motor is a DC motor turned inside out: permanent magnets on the rotor, three-phase windings on the stator, and electronic six-step commutation from an inverter bridge.
  • BLDC motors need rotor position information, from three Hall sensors or from back-EMF sensing in sensorless drives. Speed and torque are controlled by PWM.
  • BLDC advantages are no brush wear, higher efficiency and power density, quiet operation and long life; the disadvantage is that an electronic controller is essential.
  • A PMDC motor uses permanent magnet stator poles, so the flux is constant. Torque T = KtIa and back EMF Eb = Keω, giving a straight-line speed-torque characteristic.
  • PMDC motors are cheap, compact and easy to control, but they have brush wear, cannot be field weakened and can be permanently demagnetised by a stall.
  • PMDC motors are almost always used with a gearbox in robotics and automotive accessory drives.
