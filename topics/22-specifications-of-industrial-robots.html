22Specifications of Different Industrial Robots

How to read an industrial robot datasheet: degrees of freedom, payload, reach, accuracy, repeatability, speed, workspace, weight, protection class, and the specifications of real robot families.

1.Why Specifications Matter

Selecting a robot for a task is an engineering decision that must be made from the datasheet. Choosing a robot that is too small means it cannot carry the tool and the part; choosing one that is too large wastes money, floor space and energy. The following parameters must always be checked, in this order of importance: payload, reach, repeatability, speed, number of axes, workspace shape, weight, protection class and power requirement.

2.Important Specifications Explained

Number of axes / degrees of freedom

The number of independently controlled motions. A standard industrial robot has six axes: three for the arm (to place the tool centre point anywhere in the workspace) and three for the wrist (to orient the tool in any direction). Six axes are the minimum needed to position and orient a rigid body arbitrarily in space. Robots with four axes (SCARA) are used where only vertical orientation is needed. Robots with seven or more axes are called redundant; the extra axis allows the robot to reach around obstacles or to avoid singularities while keeping the tool position fixed. Additional axes may come from a rail (a seventh axis), a positioner that rotates the workpiece, or a gantry.

Payload (load capacity)

The maximum mass the robot can carry at the wrist flange, at the maximum reach and at the rated speed, usually in kilograms. It must include the weight of the end effector, the tooling, the cables and hoses, and the workpiece. Engineers usually add a safety margin of 20 % to 30 %. Payload classes are roughly: micro (under 3 kg), small (3 to 10 kg), medium (10 to 50 kg), large (50 to 250 kg) and heavy (over 250 kg, up to 2300 kg for the largest palletising robots).

Important

Exceeding the payload, or mounting a heavy tool far from the flange, reduces the accuracy, increases wear and can trigger an overload fault. The moment of inertia of the load about the wrist axes is often specified separately and is just as important as the mass.

Reach (arm span / working radius)

The maximum horizontal distance from the axis of joint 1 to the centre of the wrist flange, with the arm fully extended, in millimetres. Typical values range from 300 mm for a small assembly robot to over 3500 mm for a palletising robot and 4000 mm or more for heavy welding robots. Reach must cover the fixture, the part and any safety clearance, but a robot should not be run at its maximum reach for normal work because accuracy and rigidity are worst there.

Workspace (work envelope)

The three-dimensional volume that the wrist flange centre point can reach. Its shape depends on the configuration (Topic 18). It is specified by the maximum reach, by the dead zone (the volume the robot cannot reach, usually close to and below the base), and by the vertical and horizontal limits. The workspace is normally shown as a cross-section drawing in the datasheet.

Accuracy and repeatability

These two are different and are often confused.

  • Accuracy (pose accuracy) is the difference between the position the robot was commanded to reach and the position it actually reaches. It is affected by errors in the kinematic model, by backlash, by deflection under load and by thermal expansion. Typical values are 0.1 mm to 1 mm.
  • Repeatability (pose repeatability) is the ability to return to the same taught position repeatedly. It is a measure of scatter, not of correctness, and it is always much better than accuracy. Typical values are ±0.02 mm for a SCARA, ±0.05 mm for a medium articulated robot and ±0.2 mm for a heavy palletising robot.
  • Resolution is the smallest movement the control system can command and detect, limited by the encoder count and by the controller's arithmetic.

Because repeatability is far better than accuracy, industrial practice is to teach the robot at the actual workpiece rather than to calculate positions from a CAD model. Where absolute accuracy is needed (for example in machining), the robot must be calibrated using a laser tracker and a compensation table in the controller.

Speed and cycle time

Speeds are quoted as maximum joint speed in degrees per second for each axis, and as maximum tool speed in metres per second (typically 1 m/s to 10 m/s). For pick-and-place work the useful figure is the cycle time, measured over a standard gate movement of 25 mm up, 305 mm across and 25 mm down with a specified payload. Delta robots achieve cycle times below 0.3 seconds.

Other specifications

  • Weight of the manipulator — determines the foundation and the floor loading, and matters for rail or wall mounting.
  • Mounting options — floor, wall, inverted (ceiling), angled, or on a linear rail.
  • Protection class (IP rating) — IP54 for general industry, IP67 for washdown and foundry use. Cleanroom robots are rated to ISO Class 4 or 5.
  • Explosion protection — ATEX or pressurised (positive pressure purge) versions for painting and for flammable atmospheres.
  • Power supply — typically 3-phase 200 V to 480 V, 50/60 Hz, with a rated power of 1 kVA to 15 kVA.
  • Noise level — usually below 75 dB(A).
  • Repeatability of the wrist orientation — quoted in degrees.
  • Brakes — all axes should have fail-safe brakes that hold the arm when power is removed.
  • Controller features — programming language, number of I/O points, fieldbus support (EtherNet/IP, PROFINET, EtherCAT, DeviceNet, CC-Link), vision integration, force control option, safety functions (safe speed, safe zone, safe tool orientation), off-line programming support.
  • Environmental limits — operating temperature 0 °C to 45 °C typically, humidity limits.
  • Mean time between failures (MTBF) — typically 60 000 to 100 000 hours for a modern industrial robot.

4.How to Select a Robot for a Task

  1. Define the task precisely. What is to be moved, welded, painted or assembled? What is the cycle time required? What is the quality standard?
  2. Determine the total mass at the wrist. Add the workpiece, the gripper or tool, the mounting plate, the cables and hoses. Add a 25 % margin.
  3. Determine the required reach. Draw the cell to scale, including the fixture, the machine, the conveyor, the safety fences and any obstacles. The reach must cover the furthest point plus clearance.
  4. Determine the required accuracy and repeatability. A peg-in-hole assembly may need ±0.02 mm; palletising may only need ±0.5 mm.
  5. Decide the number of axes. Four axes are enough if the tool only needs vertical orientation; six axes are needed for arbitrary orientation; seven or more if obstacles must be avoided.
  6. Check the speed and cycle time against the production requirement.
  7. Check the environment. Washdown needs IP67; painting needs explosion protection; cleanroom needs a certified low-particle robot; foundry work needs a foundry package with a protective suit.
  8. Check the controller. Programming language, I/O count, fieldbus compatibility with the existing machines, vision and force control options, safety functions, off-line programming support, and availability of service and spares.
  9. Check the mounting and the floor loading, and whether a rail or positioner is needed.
  10. Evaluate the total cost — not only the robot, but the end effector, the safety fencing, the integration, the programming, the training, the spare parts and the maintenance contract.
  11. Prefer a standard robot over a special one, unless the task genuinely demands otherwise, because standard robots have better support and lower cost.
Example

Selection example. A CNC lathe must be loaded and unloaded every 45 seconds. The workpiece is a 6 kg steel flange, the chuck is 900 mm from the robot base, and the gripper weighs 4 kg. Total wrist load = 6 + 4 = 10 kg, plus 25 % margin = 12.5 kg. Required reach: 900 mm to the chuck plus 300 mm inside the machine plus clearance = about 1400 mm. Required repeatability: about ±0.1 mm is adequate because the chuck centre aligns the part. Environment: oil mist and chips, so an IP67 machine-tending package is advisable. Conclusion: a medium articulated 6-axis robot of 12 kg to 20 kg payload with a 1500 mm to 1800 mm reach is suitable. A 4-axis SCARA would be cheaper but cannot orient the part to present it to the chuck, so 6 axes are required.

★Summary

  • The main robot specifications are number of axes, payload, reach, workspace, accuracy, repeatability, speed, weight, protection class, mounting and power supply.
  • Six axes are the minimum needed to place and orient a rigid body arbitrarily; four axes suit SCARA assembly and seven or more give redundancy.
  • Payload must include the tool, the workpiece, the mounting hardware and the cables, with a safety margin of 20 % to 30 %.
  • Accuracy is the difference between the commanded and the actual position (0.1 mm to 1 mm); repeatability is the scatter of repeated returns to a taught position (±0.02 mm to ±0.2 mm).
  • Because repeatability is much better than accuracy, positions are taught at the workpiece rather than computed from CAD, unless the robot is calibrated.
  • Protection class, explosion protection and cleanroom rating must match the environment.
  • Robot families are micro, small, medium, large and heavy articulated, SCARA, Delta, Cartesian, collaborative, welding, painting, cleanroom and mobile manipulators.
  • Selection follows the order: task, wrist load, reach, accuracy, axes, speed, environment, controller, mounting and total cost.
  • Mean time between failures of a modern industrial robot is typically 60 000 to 100 000 hours.
